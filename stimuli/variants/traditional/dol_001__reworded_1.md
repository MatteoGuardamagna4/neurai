# Explanation

Picture two companies with identical sales and identical profit. Hit them both with a bad quarter and one barely notices while the other is badly hurt. Operating leverage explains why, and it comes down to how much of each cost base is fixed. When a firm's costs rise and fall with sales, a dip in sales costs it little, because its costs drop as well. When a firm carries heavy fixed costs, it keeps paying them regardless, so the very same dip bites much harder into profit.

The degree of operating leverage turns that sensitivity into a number. It sets what all the sales contribute against what is left once the fixed costs are paid, and you read it as a multiplier: a figure of 3 means each 1% change in sales shifts operating profit by 3%, up in a good year and down in a bad one. For a profitable firm it is always above one, since contribution is always bigger than the profit left after fixed costs.

Here is the ratio:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A workshop sells 5,000 units at EUR 40 each, with a variable cost of EUR 24, and its fixed costs are EUR 40,000. Contribution = 5,000 x 16 = EUR 80,000. Operating profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = EUR 40,000. Degree of operating leverage = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00, so sales up 10% means profit up 20%.

The usual slip is flipping the ratio. Profit divided by contribution gives a number under one, which cannot multiply anything; it actually tells you what share of contribution is left after the fixed costs. The check is easy: leverage is about amplification, so an answer under one means you divided the wrong way. Contribution on top, operating profit below, and expect more than one. The bigger the number, the more a small move in sales matters.

# Problem

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units a year at EUR 50 each, with a variable cost of EUR 30 per unit and fixed costs of EUR 120,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? Try it on your own. If you get it wrong, you will get up to three hints, one at a time, and a new attempt after each.

# Hints

Hint 1: Operating leverage compares two amounts the business already knows: what all its sales contribute, and what remains of that after the fixed costs. Find both before you build any ratio.

Hint 2: Total contribution is units sold times the margin on each one. Operating profit is that contribution minus the fixed costs, so it is always the smaller figure.

Hint 3: The degree of operating leverage is contribution divided by operating profit, the bigger over the smaller, so for a profitable firm the answer is always above one.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin per unit is 50 - 30 = EUR 20, so total contribution is 10,000 x 20 = EUR 200,000. Step 2: operating profit is 200,000 - 120,000 = EUR 80,000. Step 3: the degree of operating leverage is 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.50. Read it as a multiplier: sales up 10% would raise operating profit by 25%, and sales down 10% would cut it by the same amount. Dividing the other way would give 0.40, the share of contribution left after the fixed costs, a different and far less useful figure.
