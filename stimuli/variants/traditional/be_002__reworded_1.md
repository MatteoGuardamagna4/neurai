# Explanation

Any business that sells something has a line: below it the business loses money, above it the business earns money. Break-even analysis locates that line. You need three figures, and you have to keep them apart: the costs that stay put no matter what, the money a single sale brings in, and the cost that one more sale adds. Rent, a licence and insurance go in the first bucket; ingredients and packaging go in the third.

What separates the price from the variable cost is the contribution margin, and it is the only piece of a sale that can pay for anything else. If a meal sells for EUR 12 and uses EUR 4.50 of ingredients, it does not put EUR 12 towards the rent. It puts EUR 7.50, since the remaining EUR 4.50 has already gone into the meal.

Divide the fixed costs by that margin and you get the volume at which they are covered exactly:

Break-even quantity = Fixed costs / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A bike repair shop spends EUR 2,400 a month on its workshop, tools and insurance. It charges EUR 50 for a standard service, which takes EUR 20 of parts and consumables. Contribution margin = 50 - 20 = EUR 30 per service. Break-even = 2,400 / 30 = 80 services a month.

The error that hurts businesses most is dividing by the price. For the repair shop that would mean 2,400 / 50 = 48 services, and a shop doing 48 services a month would come nowhere close to paying for its workshop, because the EUR 20 of parts on every job has silently disappeared. Subtract first, divide second, and never swap them. It is always two steps in that order: work out what one sale leaves over, then count how many of those you need to cover the fixed costs.

# Problem

A food truck pays EUR 3,600 a month for its pitch, licence and insurance. A meal is sold at EUR 12 and costs EUR 4.50 in ingredients and packaging. How many meals must it sell each month to break even? Try it on your own. If you get it wrong, you will get up to three hints, one at a time, and a new attempt after each.

# Hints

Hint 1: Three numbers count here, and each one has its own job: what the truck pays regardless of sales, what one meal brings in, and what one meal costs to make. Label them before you calculate.

Hint 2: Only some of the EUR 12 is available for the pitch and the licence, because EUR 4.50 of it already went into the meal. Work out what one sale leaves over.

Hint 3: Divide the monthly fixed costs by that leftover amount. That gives the number of meals that covers them exactly.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the fixed costs are EUR 3,600 a month, the price is EUR 12 and the variable cost is EUR 4.50 per meal. Step 2: the contribution margin is 12 - 4.50 = EUR 7.50, what each sale leaves once the meal is paid for. Step 3: 3,600 / 7.50 = 480 meals a month, or 16 meals a day over a 30-day month. Dividing by the price would give 3,600 / 12 = 300 meals, and a truck that sold 300 meals would still fall EUR 1,350 short of its fixed costs.
