# Explanation

A profit target becomes a sales target only once taxation has been taken into account, and doing so is a brief step that is readily omitted. In the absence of tax the calculation is the break-even calculation with an additional term: the sales required are the fixed costs plus the desired profit, divided by the margin on a single sale. Each sale contributes its margin first to the fixed costs and, once these are covered, to profit.

Taxation determines which profit figure belongs in that numerator. The tax authority takes its share of the profit the business earns, so the amount retained by the owner is smaller than the amount the business generated. A target expressed as retained profit must therefore be converted upwards into the pre-tax profit that yields it, and the conversion is a division by one minus the tax rate, never a multiplication.

With the conversion incorporated:

Quantity = (Fixed costs + After-tax target / (1 - Tax rate)) / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A pottery studio incurs fixed costs of EUR 30,000 per year. A workshop place sells for EUR 120 and costs EUR 45 in clay, glaze and firing. The studio pays tax at 25% and its owner requires EUR 15,000 of profit after tax. Pre-tax profit required = 15,000 / 0.75 = EUR 20,000. Margin = 120 - 45 = EUR 75. Places = (30,000 + 20,000) / 75 = 666.67, hence 667 places.

The characteristic error is to insert the retained figure directly into the numerator. For the pottery studio this would give (30,000 + 15,000) / 75 = 600 places; at 600 places the pre-tax profit is precisely EUR 15,000, one quarter of which is paid in tax, leaving the owner EUR 11,250 instead of the EUR 15,000 required. The target must be grossed up first, and only then treated as though it were an additional fixed cost. Gross up, add to the fixed costs, divide by the margin. Omitting the first step invariably understates the volume required.

# Problem

A training studio has fixed costs of EUR 96,000 a year. A course sells for EUR 400 and costs EUR 160 to run. The studio pays tax at 20% on its profits and wants to earn EUR 48,000 after tax. How many courses must it sell? Solve it independently. Should your answer be incorrect, up to three hints will follow, one at a time, each followed by a further attempt.

# Hints

Hint 1: Tax is levied on the profit the studio earns, so the figure that determines sales volume is the profit before tax, not the amount ultimately retained by the owner.

Hint 2: If the tax authority takes one fifth of the pre-tax profit, the owner retains four fifths of it. Recover the pre-tax figure by dividing the target by one minus the tax rate.

Hint 3: Treat that pre-tax profit as though it were an additional fixed cost: add it to the EUR 96,000 and divide the total by the margin on one course.

# Worked solution

Step 1: gross up the target. At a 20% tax rate the owner retains 80% of the pre-tax profit, so 48,000 / 0.8 = EUR 60,000 must be earned before tax. Step 2: the margin on a course is 400 - 160 = EUR 240. Step 3: the courses must cover the fixed costs and that pre-tax profit: (96,000 + 60,000) / 240 = 650 courses. Adding the after-tax target directly to the fixed costs would give 600 courses, and at that volume the tax liability leaves the owner with only EUR 38,400.
