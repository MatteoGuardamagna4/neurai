# Explanation

Every pricing decision depends on an assumption about how customers will respond, and price elasticity expresses that assumption as a number. It compares the movement in quantity with the movement in price, and the comparison is valid only in percentage terms. Raw amounts cannot meaningfully be divided by one another: cups per euro is not a property of demand, since identical demand would yield an entirely different figure were the price quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured relative to the starting position. If sales decline from 1,000 to 900, the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price increases from EUR 4 to EUR 5, the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of these two percentages is the elasticity, which is conventionally reported as a positive number although price and quantity move in opposite directions.

Formally:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery increases the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales decline from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unaltered.

The characteristic error is to divide the absolute change in quantity by the absolute change in price. For the bakery this would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure devoid of meaning outside that particular pair of units. A second, subtler variant measures both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, producing a different result for a price increase than for the identical reduction reversed. The starting value is always the base. Percentages appear on both sides, each measured against the initial value. A value below one indicates inelastic demand, so a price increase will raise revenue; a value above one indicates the opposite.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Solve it independently. Should your answer be incorrect, up to three hints will follow, one at a time, each followed by a further attempt.

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity compares two percentage changes, never two absolute amounts: cups and euros cannot meaningfully be divided by one another.

Hint 2: Calculate each percentage separately relative to its starting value: the proportion of the original 1,000 cups lost, and the proportion added to the original EUR 4.00.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price. A result below one indicates that demand responds less than proportionately.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change as a percentage of its starting value: 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Step 2: the price change on the same basis: 1.00 / 4.00 = 25%. Step 3: divide: 20% / 25% = 0.80. Demand is inelastic, since quantity changed proportionately less than price, and the shop's revenue in fact increases: 800 cups at EUR 5 yields EUR 4,000 against EUR 4,000 previously, before allowing for the cups it no longer needs to produce. Dividing the absolute changes would give 200 cups per euro, a figure that would alter entirely were the price quoted in cents.
