# Explanation

A special order is a one-off piece of business that a company can take or turn down, and deciding well means putting a single question to every cost on the page: does taking the order change it? Costs that move with the decision are relevant to it. Costs that will be paid whatever the company decides are not, no matter how carefully the costing system spreads them over individual units.

Allocated fixed overhead is the usual trap. The accounts spread rent, salaries and depreciation across the units produced so each carries a share, but the rent does not go up because one more order was accepted. The opposite case matters just as much: a cost that only exists because of the order, such as a special die or an extra shift, is fully relevant even though the accounts list it under fixed costs.

For an order that can be made with spare capacity:

Incremental profit = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost) - Incremental fixed costs

Worked example. A print shop with idle presses is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink; the shop also allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster, and the job needs a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. The allocated EUR 2 is paid whether or not the order is taken, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 2,000 x (7 - 4) - 1,500 = 6,000 - 1,500 = EUR 4,500. Charging the allocated overhead would have shown 2,000 x 1 - 1,500 = EUR 500 and made a good order look marginal.

The slip is comparing the offered price with the full unit cost, variable plus allocated overhead. At the print shop that sets EUR 7 against a EUR 6 cost and makes the job look barely worth doing; had the allocated share been EUR 4, the shop would have turned the order down and finished the month EUR 4,500 worse off, with idle presses and the same overhead to pay. Judge the order on the costs it changes. One test settles every item: would this cost be any different tomorrow if the order were refused? Only then does it go into the calculation.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? Try it on your own. If you get it wrong, you will get up to three hints, one at a time, and a new attempt after each.

# Hints

Hint 1: A cost belongs here only if taking the order changes it. Go through the three costs you were given and mark each one as changed or unchanged by the decision.

Hint 2: The allocated overhead is unchanged: the same total is paid either way, and spreading it over more units does not create a new cost. The tooling charge is different, since it only exists if the order is taken.

Hint 3: Multiply the units by the contribution margin per unit, price minus variable cost, then subtract the one-off cost the order causes.

# Worked solution

Step 1: sort the costs. The EUR 22 variable cost is caused by the order, so it is relevant. The EUR 10 allocated overhead is paid anyway, so it is not. The EUR 15,000 tooling charge only exists if the order is taken, so it is relevant. Step 2: contribution per unit = 34 - 22 = EUR 12. Step 3: total contribution = 5,000 x 12 = EUR 60,000. Step 4: subtract the incremental fixed cost: 60,000 - 15,000 = EUR 45,000 of incremental profit, so the order is worth taking. Charging the allocated overhead too would show a loss of EUR 5,000 and lead the company to turn away profitable business.
