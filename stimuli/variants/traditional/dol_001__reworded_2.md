# Explanation

Two companies with identical sales and identical profit may respond quite differently to an adverse quarter, and operating leverage accounts for the difference. The determining factor is the proportion of the cost base that is fixed. A firm whose costs vary with sales loses relatively little when sales decline, since its costs decline as well. A firm with substantial fixed costs continues to bear them regardless, so an identical decline in sales reduces profit far more severely.

The degree of operating leverage quantifies this sensitivity. It relates the total contribution of sales to the amount remaining after the fixed costs, and it is interpreted as a multiplier: a value of 3 signifies that each 1% change in sales alters operating profit by 3%, upwards in a favourable year and downwards in an unfavourable one. For a profitable firm it always exceeds one, since contribution always exceeds the profit remaining after fixed costs.

The ratio is defined as:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A workshop sells 5,000 units at EUR 40 with a variable cost of EUR 24, and incurs fixed costs of EUR 40,000. Contribution = 5,000 x 16 = EUR 80,000. Operating profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = EUR 40,000. Degree of operating leverage = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00, so a 10% increase in sales raises profit by 20%.

The characteristic error is to invert the ratio. Dividing profit by contribution produces a value below one, which cannot function as a multiplier and in fact represents the share of contribution that remains after the fixed costs. The verification is straightforward: leverage measures amplification, so a result below one indicates that the division has been reversed. Contribution forms the numerator and operating profit the denominator, and a value above one is to be expected. The higher the value, the greater the effect of a small change in sales.

# Problem

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units a year at EUR 50 each, with a variable cost of EUR 30 per unit and fixed costs of EUR 120,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? Solve it independently. Should your answer be incorrect, up to three hints will follow, one at a time, each followed by a further attempt.

# Hints

Hint 1: Operating leverage relates two quantities already available to the business: the total contribution of its sales, and the amount remaining after the fixed costs. Determine both before forming any ratio.

Hint 2: Total contribution equals the units sold multiplied by the margin on each. Operating profit equals that contribution less the fixed costs, and is therefore always the smaller of the two.

Hint 3: The degree of operating leverage is contribution divided by operating profit, the larger divided by the smaller, so the result always exceeds one for a profitable firm.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin per unit is 50 - 30 = EUR 20, so total contribution is 10,000 x 20 = EUR 200,000. Step 2: operating profit is 200,000 - 120,000 = EUR 80,000. Step 3: the degree of operating leverage is 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.50. It is interpreted as a multiplier: a 10% increase in sales would raise operating profit by 25%, and a 10% decrease would reduce it by the same proportion. Inverting the division would yield 0.40, the share of contribution remaining after the fixed costs, a different and considerably less informative figure.
