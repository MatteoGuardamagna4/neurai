# Explanation

Software firms are known for lurching from heavy losses to big profits on fairly small changes in sales, and the cause is arithmetic, not luck. Nearly all their cost is fixed and spent before the first copy is sold, while each extra licence is cheap to deliver. Put those together and profit becomes extremely sensitive to volume: the fixed costs have to be paid regardless, so every sale past break-even falls almost wholly into profit, and every lost sale takes nearly its whole margin away.

The degree of operating leverage measures that sensitivity as a multiplier. It is total contribution divided by operating profit, and since contribution is the bigger of the two, it is always above one for a profitable firm. A value of 4 says that sales down 5% would cut operating profit by 20%, which is why a highly leveraged business needs a wider margin of safety than one that is not.

In symbols:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A consultancy bills 600 days a year at EUR 900 a day. Every billed day costs EUR 300 in contractor fees, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 240,000. Contribution = 600 x 600 = EUR 360,000. Operating profit = 360,000 - 240,000 = EUR 120,000. Leverage = 360,000 / 120,000 = 3.00: billings moving 10% move profit 30%.

The slip is turning the ratio over. Operating profit divided by contribution always comes out under one and cannot amplify anything; it gives the fraction of contribution that survives the fixed costs, a real number that answers another question. If a leverage calculation gives you less than one, you have swapped the two figures. The bigger figure goes on top. A high result is neither good nor bad in itself; it simply warns you how much a change in volume will count.

# Problem

A software firm sells 4,000 licences a year at EUR 250 each. Each licence costs EUR 50 in support and hosting, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 550,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? Try it on your own. If you get it wrong, you will get up to three hints, one at a time, and a new attempt after each.

# Hints

Hint 1: You need two figures first: what the licences contribute altogether, and what is left once the fixed costs are paid.

Hint 2: Contribution is the number of licences times the margin on one. Operating profit is that amount minus the EUR 550,000 of fixed costs.

Hint 3: Divide the contribution by the operating profit. A software firm with heavy fixed costs and a small variable cost will always come out high.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin per licence is 250 - 50 = EUR 200, so contribution is 4,000 x 200 = EUR 800,000. Step 2: operating profit is 800,000 - 550,000 = EUR 250,000. Step 3: leverage is 800,000 / 250,000 = 3.20. Licence sales up 10% would lift operating profit by 32%; sales down 10% would cut it by the same, which is exactly why a bad quarter hits software firms so hard. Turning the ratio over would give 0.3125, the share of contribution left after fixed costs, which answers something else.
