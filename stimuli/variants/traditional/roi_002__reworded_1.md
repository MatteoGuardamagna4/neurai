# Explanation

A machine that cost EUR 100,000 and produced EUR 160,000 has not returned 160%, and the gap between those two claims is where most ROI mistakes live. A return measures what was earned beyond the money put in, per euro put in. The cash a project generates usually includes the original outlay coming back, and handing someone their own money back is not a profit, so that part has to come out before you divide anything.

Once the gain is isolated, the bottom of the fraction is the amount that was at risk. This is the second place the calculation goes wrong: dividing the gain by the cash generated instead of by the cash invested gives a smaller and just as wrong answer, because it measures the profit against the proceeds rather than against the exposure.

Written out:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer, and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the money spent.

The slip is the money multiple: total returned divided by amount invested. For the haulier that shows 120% on a trailer that really returned 20%, and the error always flatters, which is why it lives on in pitch decks. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project that returns exactly what it cost has earned nothing, so the right method gives zero and the multiple gives one. Isolate the gain, then divide by what was committed. Any formula that shows a positive return on a project that only broke even is the wrong one.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? Try it on your own. If you get it wrong, you will get up to three hints, one at a time, and a new attempt after each.

# Hints

Hint 1: The EUR 160,000 is everything the equipment produced, and part of it is just the EUR 100,000 coming back. Find the gain first.

Hint 2: The bottom of a return is always the money put at risk, not the money that came out at the end.

Hint 3: Divide the gain by the EUR 100,000 invested and give it as a percentage.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the gain is 160,000 - 100,000 = EUR 60,000, because the first EUR 100,000 of the cash is the company's own money coming back. Step 2: divide by what was invested: 60,000 / 100,000 = 0.6, a return of 60% over the equipment's life. Reporting 160,000 / 100,000 = 160% would count the returned stake as profit and flatter the purchase badly; dividing the gain by the EUR 160,000 generated would give 37.5% and measure it against the wrong base.
