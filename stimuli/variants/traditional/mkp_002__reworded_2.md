# Explanation

An identical euro of profit may be described as a 25% markup or as a 20% margin, and a buyer who confuses the two will underprice every item in the shop. Both terms describe the difference between the cost of an item and its selling price. A markup measures that difference against the cost; a gross margin measures it against the selling price. Since the price is the larger of the two bases, the margin percentage is always the smaller figure for any given item.

The practical implication is that a target margin cannot be attained by adding that percentage to the cost. If three tenths of the price are to be gross profit, the remaining seven tenths must represent the cost, so the price equals the cost divided by 0.7. Expressing the relationship as cost equals price multiplied by one minus the margin renders the rearrangement evident and removes the temptation to multiply.

The rule is a division:

Price = Cost / (1 - Target margin)

Worked example. A bookshop purchases a hardback for EUR 45 and seeks a gross margin of 40% of the selling price. If the margin accounts for 40% of the price, the cost represents the remaining 60%: price = 45 / 0.6 = EUR 75. Verification: the profit is 75 - 45 = EUR 30, which is 40% of 75. Adding 40% to the cost would have yielded EUR 63, where the profit of EUR 18 is only 28.6% of the price.

The characteristic error is to multiply the cost by one plus the target margin. The bookshop would then charge EUR 63 in the belief that it earned 40% of the price, whereas the profit of EUR 18 represents 28.6% of it. The discrepancy widens as the target rises, which is why the error is most damaging on the items on which a shop most depends. The two measures are related by margin = markup / (1 + markup), a conversion worth committing to memory. The essential question is what the percentage is a percentage of. Against the price, divide by one minus it; against the cost, multiply.

# Problem

A boutique buys a jacket for EUR 84 and wants a gross margin of 30% of the selling price. What price should it set? Solve it independently. Should your answer be incorrect, up to three hints will follow, one at a time, each followed by a further attempt.

# Hints

Hint 1: Determine first what the 30% is a percentage of. A gross margin is always measured against the selling price, never against the amount the boutique paid.

Hint 2: If three tenths of the price are to be gross profit, then seven tenths represent the EUR 84 the jacket cost. Express this as an equation before rearranging it.

Hint 3: Divide the cost by one minus the target margin, then verify the result by dividing the profit by the price obtained.

# Worked solution

Step 1: interpret the percentage against the correct base. The margin is a share of the selling price, so the cost must represent 1 - 0.3 = 0.7 of it. Step 2: state the relationship: 84 = price x 0.7. Step 3: divide: 84 / 0.7 = EUR 120.00. Verification: the gross profit is 120 - 84 = EUR 36, and 36 / 120 is 30% of the price, precisely as required. Adding 30% to the cost would instead give EUR 109.20, where the profit of EUR 25.20 is only 23.1% of the price, since the percentage was applied to the smaller of the two bases.
