# Explanation

The payback period is the least sophisticated of the investment appraisal measures and among the most widely used: it determines how long the funds committed to a project take to be recovered. A shorter payback implies a shorter period of exposure, which explains its appeal to lenders and cautious boards, and it is readily explained to audiences unfamiliar with discounted cash flow analysis. Its limitation is that it disregards everything that occurs after the funds have been recovered.

The measure rests entirely on cash. The distinction is important because reported accounting profit is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is an accounting charge that allocates the cost of an asset over its useful life rather than a payment to any party. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the outset, and it already appears in the numerator of the calculation.

With a constant annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash per year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts report a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 per year, but no cash leaves the business in respect of that depreciation, so it must not be deducted here.

The characteristic error is to divide the investment by the accounting profit. This deducts depreciation from the annual return while the full purchase price remains in the numerator, so the cost of the asset is counted twice and the payback period is substantially overstated. Uneven cash flows require a different procedure but rest on the same principle: cumulate the cash year by year until it equals the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which recovery is completed. Cash forms both the numerator and the denominator. Any profit figure supplied should have depreciation added back before it is used here.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? Solve it independently. Should your answer be incorrect, up to three hints will follow, one at a time, each followed by a further attempt.

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback concerns how long the investment takes to return the cash committed, so both numerator and denominator must be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures provided describe the same year: one is cash, the other is the accounting result after depreciation. Depreciation is an accounting charge rather than a payment, so it has no place in a payback calculation.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow, and express the result in years.

# Worked solution

Step 1: identify the cash. The equipment generates EUR 24,000 per year. The EUR 15,000 profit figure represents that same cash after the EUR 9,000 depreciation charge, and depreciation is not paid to any party, so the cash figure is the appropriate one. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 90,000 / 24,000 = 3.75 years. The company recovers its funds three quarters of the way through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would instead give 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years, which seriously understates the investment by deducting a cost that never leaves the bank account.
