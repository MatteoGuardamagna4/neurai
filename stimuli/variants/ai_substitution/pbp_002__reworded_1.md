# Explanation

Let's revisit the payback period, this time for another investment. It is the first thing a careful owner wants to know: how long until the money comes back? It is the roughest of the appraisal measures and one of the most used, because it is quick, easy to explain and speaks straight to risk: the sooner the money comes back, the less time the business is exposed. Its weakness is that it says nothing about what comes after, so a project that pays back fast and then stops looks the same as one that pays back fast and runs for ten years.

The measure is built on cash, and that is where it usually goes wrong. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been taken out of it, and depreciation is an accounting tool that spreads an asset's cost over its life, not a payment anyone makes. The money for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it already sits in the top of the fraction waiting to be recovered.

With the same cash flow every year:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry buys a EUR 45,000 machine that brings in EUR 15,000 of cash a year. Depreciated over five years, it carries a charge of EUR 9,000 a year, so the accounts show a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, much too long, because depreciation has been taken out of a return that never actually paid it.

The slip is dividing by the reported profit. That charges the asset twice, once as the full price on top of the fraction and again as depreciation taken out of the yearly return, and the answer ends up far too gloomy. When cash flows are uneven, the division stops working altogether: add up the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally passed. Keep that in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because picking between the two yearly figures is where this goes wrong.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: choose the cash figure. The kitchen brings in EUR 24,000 a year; the EUR 14,000 is that same cash after a EUR 10,000 bookkeeping charge, and no money leaves the hotel for depreciation. Step 2: divide the investment by the yearly cash flow: 84,000 / 24,000 = 3.50 years. The hotel has its money back halfway through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would give 84,000 / 14,000 = 6.00 years, nearly twice as long, because the purchase price is already on top and the depreciation charges it a second time. The fraction has a simple meaning: three years bring back EUR 72,000, and the last EUR 12,000 comes in halfway through the fourth.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: take the yearly cash flow, ignoring depreciation and any profit figure built on it, and divide the investment by it. If the cash flows are uneven, add them up year by year and add the fraction of the year in which the investment is recovered. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
