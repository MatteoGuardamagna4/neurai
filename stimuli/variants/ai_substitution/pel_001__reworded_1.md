# Explanation

Let's go through price elasticity together. Every pricing decision rests on a guess about how customers will respond, and elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how far quantity moves with how far price moved, and the comparison only works in percentages. You cannot divide raw amounts into each other: cups per euro is not a feature of the demand, because the very same demand would give a totally different figure if the price happened to be quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured from where things began. If sales drop from 1,000 to 900, the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5, the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of those two percentages is the elasticity, and by convention it is given as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery lifts the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales drop from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue stays the same.

The slip is dividing the raw change in quantity by the raw change in price. For the bakery that would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure with no meaning outside that particular pair of units. A second, subtler version measures both changes against the new values instead of the original ones, which gives a different answer for a price rise than for the same price cut in reverse. Always divide by where you started. Keep that in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because dividing raw amounts is the slip people make most.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change as a percentage of where it began: 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Step 2: the price change the same way: 1.00 / 4.00 = 25%. Step 3: divide: 20% / 25% = 0.80. Demand is inelastic, since quantity moved proportionately less than price, and the shop's revenue actually rises: 800 cups at EUR 5 is EUR 4,000 against EUR 4,000 before, before counting the cups it no longer has to make. Dividing the raw changes would give 200 cups per euro, a figure that would change completely if the price were quoted in cents. Here is the practical reading: an elasticity below one means the price rise was worth it, because revenue holds up even though fewer cups are sold.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: write the quantity change as a percentage of the starting quantity, the price change as a percentage of the starting price, then divide the first by the second. To predict a change, multiply the planned price change by the elasticity. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
