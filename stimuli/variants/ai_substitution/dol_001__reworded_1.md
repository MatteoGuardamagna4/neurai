# Explanation

Let's go through operating leverage together. Picture two companies with identical sales and profit: hit them both with a bad quarter and one barely notices while the other is badly hurt. It comes down to how much of each cost base is fixed. When a firm's costs rise and fall with sales, a dip in sales costs it little, because its costs drop as well. When a firm carries heavy fixed costs, it keeps paying them regardless, so the very same dip bites much harder into profit.

The degree of operating leverage turns that sensitivity into a number. It sets what all the sales contribute against what is left once the fixed costs are paid, and you read it as a multiplier: a figure of 3 means each 1% change in sales shifts operating profit by 3%, up in a good year and down in a bad one. For a profitable firm it is always above one, since contribution is always bigger than the profit left after fixed costs.

Here is the ratio:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A workshop sells 5,000 units at EUR 40 each, with a variable cost of EUR 24, and its fixed costs are EUR 40,000. Contribution = 5,000 x 16 = EUR 80,000. Operating profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = EUR 40,000. Degree of operating leverage = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00, so sales up 10% means profit up 20%.

The usual slip is flipping the ratio. Profit divided by contribution gives a number under one, which cannot multiply anything; it actually tells you what share of contribution is left after the fixed costs. The check is easy: leverage is about amplification, so an answer under one means you divided the wrong way. Keep that check in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because flipping the ratio is the slip people make most.

# Problem

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units a year at EUR 50 each, with a variable cost of EUR 30 per unit and fixed costs of EUR 120,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin per unit is 50 - 30 = EUR 20, so total contribution is 10,000 x 20 = EUR 200,000. Step 2: operating profit is 200,000 - 120,000 = EUR 80,000. Step 3: the degree of operating leverage is 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.50. Read it as a multiplier: sales up 10% would raise operating profit by 25%, and sales down 10% would cut it by the same amount. Dividing the other way would give 0.40, the share of contribution left after the fixed costs, a different and far less useful figure. Note how to read the answer: it is a multiplier, not a percentage, so a 10% swing in sales turns into a 25% swing in profit, whichever way sales go.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: find total contribution as units times the margin on one, take away the fixed costs to get operating profit, then divide contribution by profit. Multiply the result by any percentage change in sales to get the change in profit. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
