# Explanation

Let's revisit customer lifetime value, this time for another business. A subscription company does not make its money on the first sale, so that first payment badly undervalues a customer. Lifetime value is what one customer is worth over the whole relationship, and it is the number that tells you how much it makes sense to spend winning them. It turns a marketing budget from an expense into an investment whose return you can check.

It is made of two pieces, and both are easy to get slightly wrong. The first is what the company keeps each period, a margin rather than a price: a EUR 80 subscription that costs EUR 32 to fulfil leaves EUR 48, and only that EUR 48 can cover acquisition, overheads and profit. The second is how long the customer stays, which comes from churn: when a steady share leaves each month, the average life is one divided by that share, so 5% monthly churn means twenty months and 10% means ten.

Put them together:

Lifetime value = Period revenue x Gross margin / Churn rate for that period

Worked example. A magazine charges EUR 15 a month. Printing, postage and editorial take 20% of that, leaving an 80% gross margin, and 4% of subscribers cancel every month. Monthly margin = 15 x 0.8 = EUR 12. The average subscriber stays 1 / 0.04 = 25 months. Lifetime value = 12 x 25 = EUR 300.

The slip is building the figure on revenue. For the magazine that gives 15 / 0.04 = EUR 375 rather than EUR 300, and the gap grows as margins shrink: a business keeping just a third of its revenue would overvalue every customer three times over. Another form of the slip assumes everyone stays exactly a year, which discards precisely what churn tells you and hides any gain from better retention. Keep that in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because applying the margin is the step people skip most.

# Problem

A meal-kit company charges EUR 80 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 60% of that revenue, and 8% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the monthly gross margin is 80 x 0.6 = EUR 48, what is left once the boxes are packed and delivered. Step 2: with 8% cancelling every month, a customer stays 1 / 0.08 = 12.5 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 48 x 12.5 = EUR 600, the same as dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate. Using the full EUR 80 of revenue would give EUR 1,000 and would let the company spend on acquisition money already set aside for ingredients and couriers. See how lifetime value reacts far more to churn than to price: halve the cancellation rate and the answer doubles, while a ten per cent price rise moves it by just a tenth.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: multiply the period revenue by the gross margin to get what the company keeps, then divide by the churn rate for that same period. If the question gives an average life instead of a churn rate, multiply by it rather than dividing. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
