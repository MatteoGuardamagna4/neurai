# Explanation

Let's go through the payback period together. It is the roughest of the investment appraisal tools and also one of the most popular: it asks how long it takes for the money put into a project to come back. A shorter payback means the company is at risk for less time, which is why lenders and careful boards like it, and it is easy to explain to people who would never sit through a discounted cash flow. Its weakness is that it ignores everything that happens once the money has been recovered.

The measure is built entirely on cash. That matters because the profit in the accounts is not cash: depreciation has been subtracted from it, and depreciation is a bookkeeping charge that spreads an asset's cost over its life, not a payment to anyone. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it already sits in the top of the calculation.

With the same cash flow every year:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and should save EUR 16,000 of cash a year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts show a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 a year, but no cash leaves the business for that depreciation, so it must not be subtracted here.

The slip is dividing the investment by the accounting profit. That takes depreciation off the yearly return while the full purchase price still sits on top, so the asset's cost is counted twice and the payback comes out far too long. Uneven cash flows need a different method but rest on the same idea: add up the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which it is finally recovered. Keep that in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because picking between the two yearly figures is where people slip most.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the cash. The equipment brings in EUR 24,000 a year. The EUR 15,000 profit figure is the same cash after the EUR 9,000 depreciation charge, and depreciation is not paid to anyone, so the cash figure is the right one. Step 2: divide the investment by the yearly cash flow: 90,000 / 24,000 = 3.75 years. The company has its money back three quarters of the way through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would give 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years, which badly undervalues the investment by subtracting a cost that never leaves the bank account. The fraction has a plain meaning: three full years bring back EUR 72,000, and the last EUR 18,000 comes in three quarters of the way through the fourth year.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: take the yearly cash flow, ignoring depreciation and any profit figure built on it, and divide the investment by it. If the cash flows are uneven, add them up year by year and add the fraction of the year in which the investment is finally recovered. You don't need to check it another way first; just follow the steps as shown.
