# Explanation

Let us examine return on investment together. It is the most frequently cited figure in business and the most frequently miscalculated, almost invariably in the same direction. It addresses a single question: for each euro committed, how much was returned in addition to that euro? The words "in addition to" constitute the entire difficulty, since the funds a project returns generally include the original stake, and the stake was never profit.

The calculation therefore comprises two steps that must not be combined. First, distinguish the gain from the stake: if EUR 400,000 is invested and EUR 500,000 is received, the gain is EUR 100,000 and the remainder is merely the return of the company's own funds. Second, divide that gain by the amount placed at risk, since the denominator of any return is the amount committed rather than the amount received.

Formally:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A shop spends EUR 50,000 on a refit, which generates EUR 65,000 of additional gross profit over its life. The gain is 65,000 - 50,000 = EUR 15,000. ROI = 15,000 / 50,000 = 0.3, a return of 30% on the funds invested.

The characteristic error is to divide the total returned by the amount invested and describe the result as a return. That figure is the money multiple: it treats the stake as profit and renders a respectable project spectacular on paper. A less conspicuous variant divides the gain by the total received rather than by the investment, which understates the return. A useful verification is that a project which merely breaks even must show a return of zero, not of one. Bear this verification in mind as I present the calculation below, since the stake is what is most often counted twice.

# Problem

A company invests EUR 400,000 in a project and receives EUR 500,000 back in total over the project's life. What is the return on investment? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: distinguish the stake from the gain. Of the EUR 500,000 received, EUR 400,000 is the return of the company's own funds, so the gain is 500,000 - 400,000 = EUR 100,000. Step 2: divide the gain by the amount invested: 100,000 / 400,000 = 0.25, a return of 25%. Reporting 500,000 / 400,000 = 125% would present the same project as five times better than it is, since it treats the returned stake as profit. Dividing by the EUR 500,000 received would instead give 20%, which measures the gain relative to the proceeds rather than to the risk assumed. Note the verification worth applying on every occasion: a project that returns exactly its cost yields a return of zero, so any method reporting 100% in that case is incorrect.

Apply these two steps for the remainder of the unit: subtract the investment from the total returned to isolate the gain, then divide that gain by the investment. Where a question spans several years and requires an annual figure, divide once more by the number of years. Apply the steps exactly as presented.
