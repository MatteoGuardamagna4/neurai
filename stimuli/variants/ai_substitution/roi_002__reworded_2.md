# Explanation

Let us return to return on investment, applied to a different purchase. A machine that cost EUR 100,000 and generated EUR 160,000 has not yielded a return of 160%. A return measures the amount earned beyond the funds committed, per euro committed. The cash generated by a project ordinarily includes the recovery of the original outlay, and the return of one's own funds does not constitute profit, so that portion must be removed before any division is performed.

Once the gain has been isolated, the denominator is the amount placed at risk. This is the second point at which the calculation may err: dividing the gain by the cash generated rather than by the cash invested produces a smaller and equally incorrect result, since it measures the profit against the proceeds rather than against the exposure.

Formally:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer, which earns EUR 30,000 of additional cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the funds spent.

The characteristic error is the money multiple: total returned divided by amount invested. For the haulier this reports 120% on a trailer that in fact returned 20%, and the error invariably flatters, which is why it persists in investor presentations. The decisive test is the break-even case: a project that returns exactly its cost has earned nothing, so the correct method yields zero whereas the multiple yields one. Bear the break-even test in mind as I present the calculation below, since treating the stake as profit is the customary error.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the gain is 160,000 - 100,000 = EUR 60,000, since the first EUR 100,000 of the cash represents the return of the company's own funds. Step 2: divide by the amount invested: 60,000 / 100,000 = 0.6, a return of 60% over the life of the equipment. Reporting 160,000 / 100,000 = 160% would treat the returned stake as profit and seriously flatter the acquisition; dividing the gain by the EUR 160,000 generated would give 37.5% and measure it against an inappropriate base. Observe that the two incorrect answers err in opposite directions: the multiple flatters the acquisition, whereas division by the cash generated understates it.

Apply these two steps for the remainder of the unit: subtract the investment from the total returned, then divide by the investment. To convert a required return into a target, multiply the investment by one plus that return, since the stake must also be recovered. Apply the steps exactly as presented.
