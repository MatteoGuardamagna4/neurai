# Explanation

Let's revisit a one-off job decision, this time in another business. Such a job should be judged on what it changes, and the costing system is not built to answer that. A cost is relevant to the decision only if taking the work makes it different. Costs that will be paid whatever is decided are irrelevant, however carefully the accounts share them out across jobs, and a share of a cost you cannot avoid is still a cost you cannot avoid.

Allocated overhead is the classic trap, and it catches people in both directions. Office rent and management salaries are spread across jobs so each carries a slice, which is the right way to report a job's full cost, but the rent does not go up when one more job is taken. On the other hand, a licence, a permit or a tool bought only because the job was accepted is fully relevant, even though it is a fixed amount rather than a cost per hour.

For work that fits into capacity the business already has:

Incremental profit = Price - Costs the job causes

Worked example. A photographer with a free weekend is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the job cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking this size, and the couple want a drone permit that costs EUR 150 and is bought only for this wedding. The EUR 400 of studio rent is paid whatever happens that weekend, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 1,800 - 700 - 150 = EUR 950. Charging the allocated rent would have shown EUR 550 and made a very good booking look ordinary.

The slip is comparing the offered price with the full cost the accounts report. The photographer would set EUR 1,800 against a full cost of EUR 1,250 and decide the wedding was hardly worth the weekend; had the allocated share been bigger, the booking would have been refused and the free weekend would have earned nothing. Judge the job on the cash it changes, and leave the allocation to its separate job of reporting. Keep that test in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because sorting the costs is where this goes wrong.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: sort the three costs. The EUR 22,000 of developer time and cloud capacity is caused by the job, so it counts. The EUR 12,000 of allocated overhead is paid whatever the firm decides, so it does not. The EUR 5,000 licence only exists because of the job, so it counts. Step 2: subtract only what changes: 40,000 - 22,000 - 5,000 = EUR 13,000 of incremental profit, and the job is worth taking. Charging the allocated overhead too would leave EUR 1,000 and make a solidly profitable job look like a rounding error, tempting the firm to leave its developers idle. See how the licence and the allocated overhead are both fixed amounts and still get treated differently: what counts is not whether a cost is fixed but whether the decision creates it.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: mark every cost as changed or unchanged by the decision, take away from the price only the ones that change, and leave allocated overhead out. If the business has no spare capacity, also subtract the contribution lost on the work pushed out. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
