# Explanation

Let us return to the payback period, applied to a different investment. It is the first question a prudent owner poses: how long until the funds are recovered? It is the least sophisticated of the appraisal measures and among the most frequently used, since it is rapid, readily explained and addresses risk directly: the sooner the funds return, the shorter the period of exposure. Its limitation is that it conveys nothing about subsequent events, so a project that pays back quickly and then ceases appears identical to one that pays back quickly and continues for a decade.

The measure is based on cash, and this is where it is most frequently misapplied. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is an accounting convention that allocates an asset's cost over its life rather than a payment made by anyone. The funds for the asset left the business once, at the outset, and they already appear in the numerator awaiting recovery.

With a constant annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry acquires a EUR 45,000 machine that generates EUR 15,000 of cash per year. Depreciated over five years, it bears a charge of EUR 9,000 per year, so the accounts report a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, a considerable overstatement, since the depreciation has been deducted from a return that never actually bore it.

The characteristic error is to divide by the reported profit. This charges the asset twice, once as the full price in the numerator and again as depreciation deducted from the annual return, and the result is excessively pessimistic. Where cash flows are uneven, the division ceases to apply altogether: cumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally exceeded. Bear this in mind as I present the calculation below, since the choice between the two annual figures is where the error arises.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: select the cash figure. The kitchen generates EUR 24,000 per year; the EUR 14,000 represents that same cash after a EUR 10,000 accounting charge, and no funds leave the hotel in respect of depreciation. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 84,000 / 24,000 = 3.50 years. The hotel recovers its funds halfway through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would give 84,000 / 14,000 = 6.00 years, almost twice as long, since the purchase price already appears in the numerator and the depreciation charges it a second time. The fraction has a straightforward interpretation: three years return EUR 72,000, and the remaining EUR 12,000 is received halfway through the fourth.

Apply this method for the remainder of the unit: take the annual cash flow, disregarding depreciation and any profit figure derived from it, and divide the investment by it. Where the cash flows are uneven, cumulate them year by year and add the fraction of the year in which the investment is recovered. Apply the steps exactly as presented.
