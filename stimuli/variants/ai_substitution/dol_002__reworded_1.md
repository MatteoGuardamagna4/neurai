# Explanation

Let's revisit operating leverage, this time in another industry. Software firms lurch from losses to big profits on fairly small changes in sales, and the cause is arithmetic. Nearly all their cost is fixed and spent before the first copy is sold, while each extra licence is cheap to deliver. Put those together and profit becomes extremely sensitive to volume: the fixed costs have to be paid regardless, so every sale past break-even falls almost wholly into profit, and every lost sale takes nearly its whole margin away.

The degree of operating leverage measures that sensitivity as a multiplier. It is total contribution divided by operating profit, and since contribution is the bigger of the two, it is always above one for a profitable firm. A value of 4 says that sales down 5% would cut operating profit by 20%, which is why a highly leveraged business needs a wider margin of safety than one that is not.

In symbols:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A consultancy bills 600 days a year at EUR 900 a day. Every billed day costs EUR 300 in contractor fees, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 240,000. Contribution = 600 x 600 = EUR 360,000. Operating profit = 360,000 - 240,000 = EUR 120,000. Leverage = 360,000 / 120,000 = 3.00: billings moving 10% move profit 30%.

The slip is turning the ratio over. Operating profit divided by contribution always comes out under one and cannot amplify anything; it gives the fraction of contribution that survives the fixed costs, a real number that answers another question. If a leverage calculation gives you less than one, you have swapped the two figures. Keep that in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because turning the ratio over is the slip people make most.

# Problem

A software firm sells 4,000 licences a year at EUR 250 each. Each licence costs EUR 50 in support and hosting, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 550,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin per licence is 250 - 50 = EUR 200, so contribution is 4,000 x 200 = EUR 800,000. Step 2: operating profit is 800,000 - 550,000 = EUR 250,000. Step 3: leverage is 800,000 / 250,000 = 3.20. Licence sales up 10% would lift operating profit by 32%; sales down 10% would cut it by the same, which is exactly why a bad quarter hits software firms so hard. Turning the ratio over would give 0.3125, the share of contribution left after fixed costs, which answers something else. See what the figure warns you about: with leverage above three, losing a sixth of licence sales would wipe out half of operating profit.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: multiply volume by the margin on one unit to get contribution, subtract the fixed costs for operating profit, then divide contribution by profit. Apply any percentage change in sales by multiplying it by that figure. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
