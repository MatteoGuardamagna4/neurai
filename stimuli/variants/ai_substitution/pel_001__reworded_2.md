# Explanation

Let us examine price elasticity together. Every pricing decision depends on an assumption about how customers will respond, and elasticity expresses that assumption as a number. It compares the movement in quantity with the movement in price, and the comparison is valid only in percentage terms. Raw amounts cannot meaningfully be divided by one another: cups per euro is not a property of demand, since identical demand would yield an entirely different figure were the price quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured relative to the starting position. If sales decline from 1,000 to 900, the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price increases from EUR 4 to EUR 5, the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of these two percentages is the elasticity, which is conventionally reported as a positive number although price and quantity move in opposite directions.

Formally:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery increases the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales decline from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unaltered.

The characteristic error is to divide the absolute change in quantity by the absolute change in price. For the bakery this would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure devoid of meaning outside that particular pair of units. A second, subtler variant measures both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, producing a different result for a price increase than for the identical reduction reversed. The starting value is always the base. Bear this in mind as I present the calculation below, since the division of absolute amounts is the customary error.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change as a percentage of its starting value: 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Step 2: the price change on the same basis: 1.00 / 4.00 = 25%. Step 3: divide: 20% / 25% = 0.80. Demand is inelastic, since quantity changed proportionately less than price, and the shop's revenue in fact increases: 800 cups at EUR 5 yields EUR 4,000 against EUR 4,000 previously, before allowing for the cups it no longer needs to produce. Dividing the absolute changes would give 200 cups per euro, a figure that would alter entirely were the price quoted in cents. Note the practical interpretation: an elasticity below one indicates that the price increase was justified, since revenue is maintained although fewer cups are sold.

Apply this method for the remainder of the unit: express the quantity change as a percentage of the starting quantity, the price change as a percentage of the starting price, then divide the first by the second. To forecast a change, multiply the intended price change by the elasticity. Apply the steps exactly as presented.
