# Explanation

Let us examine the payback period together. It is the least sophisticated of the investment appraisal measures and among the most widely used: it determines how long the funds committed to a project take to be recovered. A shorter payback implies a shorter period of exposure, which explains its appeal to lenders and cautious boards, and it is readily explained to audiences unfamiliar with discounted cash flow analysis. Its limitation is that it disregards everything that occurs after the funds have been recovered.

The measure rests entirely on cash. The distinction is important because reported accounting profit is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is an accounting charge that allocates the cost of an asset over its useful life rather than a payment to any party. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the outset, and it already appears in the numerator of the calculation.

With a constant annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash per year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts report a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 per year, but no cash leaves the business in respect of that depreciation, so it must not be deducted here.

The characteristic error is to divide the investment by the accounting profit. This deducts depreciation from the annual return while the full purchase price remains in the numerator, so the cost of the asset is counted twice and the payback period is substantially overstated. Uneven cash flows require a different procedure but rest on the same principle: cumulate the cash year by year until it equals the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which recovery is completed. Bear this in mind as I present the calculation below, since the choice between the two annual figures is the step most frequently mistaken.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: identify the cash. The equipment generates EUR 24,000 per year. The EUR 15,000 profit figure represents that same cash after the EUR 9,000 depreciation charge, and depreciation is not paid to any party, so the cash figure is the appropriate one. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 90,000 / 24,000 = 3.75 years. The company recovers its funds three quarters of the way through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would instead give 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years, which seriously understates the investment by deducting a cost that never leaves the bank account. The fraction has a straightforward interpretation: three full years return EUR 72,000, and the remaining EUR 18,000 is received three quarters of the way through the fourth year.

Apply this method for the remainder of the unit: take the annual cash flow, disregarding depreciation and any profit figure derived from it, and divide the investment by it. Where the cash flows are uneven, cumulate them year by year and add the fraction of the year in which the investment is finally recovered. No alternative verification is required; apply the steps exactly as presented.
