# Explanation

Let's revisit price elasticity, this time for another business. A price rise that works well for one business ruins another, depending on how strongly customers react. Elasticity measures that reaction as the ratio of two percentage changes: how far quantity moved against how far price moved. Working in percentages is not a matter of style but the only way the number can mean anything, since it removes the units and leaves a clean comparison of responsiveness.

Each percentage is taken against its own starting value. Members lost are a share of the members there were before, and the price rise is a share of the price there was before. You read the result against one: below one, demand is inelastic and total revenue rises with the price; above one, it is elastic and revenue falls; at exactly one, the two effects cancel out.

Written out:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A cinema raises its ticket price from EUR 10 to EUR 11 and weekly admissions drop from 2,000 to 1,800. Admissions fall by 200 / 2,000 = 10%. The price rises by 1 / 10 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, the unit-elastic case where revenue does not change.

The slip is dividing raw members by raw euros. For the cinema that gives 200 admissions per euro, which would turn into 2 admissions per cent for exactly the same behaviour, so it cannot be describing the customers at all. A second version measures the changes against the end values instead of the starting ones, which makes a price rise and the identical price cut give different elasticities for the same two points. Keep that comparison with one in mind as I walk you through the calculation below, because raw amounts are the slip people make most.

# Problem

A gym raises its monthly membership from EUR 20 to EUR 24 and the number of members falls from 1,000 to 700. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? There is no need to work it out yourself. If you answer wrongly, I will show you the full calculation and the answer straight away, one step at a time.

# Worked solution

Step 1: members drop by 300 out of 1,000, which is 30%. Step 2: the price rises by 4 out of 20, which is 20%. Step 3: divide: 30% / 20% = 1.50. Demand is elastic: members reacted proportionately more than the price moved, so revenue falls from EUR 20,000 to EUR 16,800 and the rise was a mistake. Dividing the raw figures would give 75 members per euro, which would become 0.75 if the price were quoted in cents, so it cannot be a property of the demand itself. See what the number says about the decision: above one, the gym is selling proportionately fewer memberships than the price went up, so the rise cost it revenue.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: take each change as a percentage of where it began, divide the quantity percentage by the price percentage, then compare the answer with one to see which way revenue moved. Follow the steps exactly as shown.
