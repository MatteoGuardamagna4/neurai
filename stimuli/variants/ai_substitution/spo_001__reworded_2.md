# Explanation

Let us examine a special order decision together. It is a single transaction that a company may accept or decline, and a sound decision requires that one question be put to every cost presented: does acceptance of the order alter it? Costs that vary with the decision are relevant to it. Costs that will be incurred irrespective of the decision are not relevant, however carefully the costing system allocates them to individual units.

Allocated fixed overhead is the customary pitfall. The accounts distribute rent, salaries and depreciation across the units produced so that each bears a share, yet the rent does not increase because an additional order has been accepted. The converse is equally important: a cost that arises solely because of the order, such as a special die or an additional shift, is wholly relevant even though the accounts classify it as fixed.

For an order that can be produced using spare capacity:

Incremental profit = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost) - Incremental fixed costs

Worked example. A print shop with idle presses is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink; the shop also allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster it prints, and the job requires a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. The allocated EUR 2 is incurred whether or not the order is accepted, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 2,000 x (7 - 4) - 1,500 = 6,000 - 1,500 = EUR 4,500. Charging the allocated overhead would have shown 2,000 x 1 - 1,500 = EUR 500 and rendered a sound order marginal.

The characteristic error is to compare the offered price with the full unit cost, comprising variable cost and allocated overhead. At the print shop this sets EUR 7 against a EUR 6 cost and makes the job appear scarcely worthwhile; had the allocated share been EUR 4, the shop would have declined the order and concluded the month EUR 4,500 worse off, with idle presses and the same overhead to meet. The order should be assessed on the costs it alters. Bear this test in mind as I present the calculation below, since the classification of costs is the step most frequently mistaken.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? It is not necessary to solve this yourself. Should your answer be incorrect, I shall present the complete calculation and the answer immediately, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: classify the costs. The EUR 22 variable cost is occasioned by the order, so it is relevant. The EUR 10 allocated overhead is incurred in any case, so it is not. The EUR 15,000 tooling charge arises only if the order is accepted, so it is relevant. Step 2: contribution per unit = 34 - 22 = EUR 12. Step 3: total contribution = 5,000 x 12 = EUR 60,000. Step 4: deduct the incremental fixed cost: 60,000 - 15,000 = EUR 45,000 of incremental profit, so the order should be accepted. Charging the allocated overhead as well would show a loss of EUR 5,000 and lead the company to decline profitable business. Observe that the tooling charge and the allocated overhead are treated differently although the accounts classify both as fixed: one is occasioned by the decision and the other is not.

Apply this method for the remainder of the unit: classify every cost as altered or unaltered by the decision, multiply the volume by the price less the relevant variable cost, then deduct only the fixed costs occasioned by the order itself. Where capacity is fully utilised, also deduct the contribution forgone on the business displaced. Apply the steps exactly as presented.
