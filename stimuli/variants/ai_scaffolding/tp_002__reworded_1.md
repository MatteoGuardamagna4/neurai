# Explanation

Let's come back to target profit after tax, this time for another business. A profit target only turns into a sales target once tax has been dealt with. Without tax the calculation is the break-even one with an extra term: the sales needed are the fixed costs plus the profit wanted, divided by the margin on one sale. Each sale contributes its margin first to the fixed costs and then, once those are paid, to profit.

Tax changes which profit figure goes into that numerator. The tax authority takes its share of the profit the business earns, so what the owner keeps is less than what the business made. A target given as take-home profit therefore has to be converted upwards into the pre-tax profit that produces it, and the conversion is a division by one minus the tax rate, never a multiplication.

With the conversion built in:

Quantity = (Fixed costs + After-tax target / (1 - Tax rate)) / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A pottery studio's fixed costs are EUR 30,000 a year. A workshop place sells for EUR 120 and costs EUR 45 in clay, glaze and firing. The studio pays tax at 25% and its owner wants EUR 15,000 of profit after tax. Pre-tax profit needed = 15,000 / 0.75 = EUR 20,000. Margin = 120 - 45 = EUR 75. Places = (30,000 + 20,000) / 75 = 666.67, so 667 places.

The slip is putting the take-home figure straight into the numerator. For the pottery studio that would give (30,000 + 15,000) / 75 = 600 places; at 600 places the pre-tax profit is exactly EUR 15,000, a quarter of which goes in tax, leaving the owner EUR 11,250 rather than the EUR 15,000 wanted. Gross up first, and only then treat the profit as if it were another fixed cost. Hold on to that first step while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what you did with the tax rate.

# Problem

A training studio has fixed costs of EUR 96,000 a year. A course sells for EUR 400 and costs EUR 160 to run. The studio pays tax at 20% on its profits and wants to earn EUR 48,000 after tax. How many courses must it sell? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

What profit does the studio need to make before tax for EUR 48,000 to be left once the tax bill is paid?

Did the tax rate go into your working as a multiplication or a division, and how can you tell which is right?

Imagine someone works out (96,000 + 48,000) / 240. Who takes a share of the profit in that plan, and how much is left?

# Hints

Hint 1: Tax is charged on the profit the studio earns, so the figure that drives sales volume is the profit before tax, not what the owner ends up keeping.

Hint 2: If the tax authority takes a fifth of the pre-tax profit, the owner keeps four fifths of it. Get back the pre-tax figure by dividing the target by one minus the tax rate.

Hint 3: Treat that pre-tax profit as if it were an extra fixed cost: add it to the EUR 96,000 and divide the total by the margin on one course.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
