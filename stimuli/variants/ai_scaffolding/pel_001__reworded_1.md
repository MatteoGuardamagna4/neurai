# Explanation

Let's figure out price elasticity together. Every pricing decision rests on a guess about how customers will respond, and elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how far quantity moves with how far price moved, and the comparison only works in percentages. You cannot divide raw amounts into each other: cups per euro is not a feature of the demand, because the very same demand would give a totally different figure if the price happened to be quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured from where things began. If sales drop from 1,000 to 900, the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5, the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of those two percentages is the elasticity, and by convention it is given as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery lifts the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales drop from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue stays the same.

The slip is dividing the raw change in quantity by the raw change in price. For the bakery that would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure with no meaning outside that particular pair of units. A second, subtler version measures both changes against the new values instead of the original ones, which gives a different answer for a price rise than for the same price cut in reverse. Always divide by where you started. Hold on to that while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what you divided by what.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

As a percentage, what share of the original 1,000 cups was lost?

As a percentage, how much was added to the original EUR 4.00 price?

Imagine someone divides 200 cups by EUR 1. What would that answer turn into if the price had been quoted in cents?

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity compares two percentage changes, never two raw amounts: dividing cups by euros gives nothing meaningful.

Hint 2: Work out each percentage on its own against its starting value: how much of the original 1,000 cups was lost, and how much was added to the original EUR 4.00.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price. An answer below one means demand reacts less than proportionately.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
