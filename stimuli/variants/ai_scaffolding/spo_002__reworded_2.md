# Explanation

Let us return to a one-off engagement decision, applied to a different business. Such an engagement should be evaluated on the basis of what it alters, and the costing system is not designed to address that question. A cost is relevant to the decision only if acceptance of the work causes it to differ. Costs that will be incurred irrespective of the decision are irrelevant, however carefully the accounts distribute them across engagements, and a share of an unavoidable cost remains unavoidable.

Allocated overhead is the classic pitfall, and it operates in both directions. Office rent and management salaries are distributed across engagements so that each bears a portion, which is the appropriate way to report the full cost of an engagement, yet the rent does not increase when one further engagement is accepted. Conversely, a licence, a permit or a tool acquired solely because the engagement was accepted is entirely relevant, even though it is a fixed amount rather than a cost per hour.

For work that can be accommodated within existing capacity:

Incremental profit = Price - Costs the job causes

Worked example. A photographer with an unoccupied weekend is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the engagement cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking of this size, and the couple require a drone permit costing EUR 150, acquired solely for this wedding. The EUR 400 of studio rent is incurred regardless of the weekend's activity, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 1,800 - 700 - 150 = EUR 950. Charging the allocated rent would have shown EUR 550 and made an excellent booking appear unremarkable.

The characteristic error is to compare the offered price with the full cost reported by the accounts. The photographer would set EUR 1,800 against a full cost of EUR 1,250 and conclude that the wedding scarcely justified the weekend; had the allocated share been larger, the booking would have been declined and the unoccupied weekend would have earned nothing. The engagement should be assessed on the cash it alters, while the allocation fulfils its separate reporting function. Bear this test in mind as you attempt the problem below; should your answer be incorrect, my first question will concern which costs you included.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? Please attempt it yourself first. I shall not disclose the answer: if your result is incorrect, I shall ask a question about your working and provide a hint, after which you may try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before commenting on your result, I would like to ask three questions.

Which of the three costs in the problem would differ next month were the firm to decline this engagement?

Does the firm cease to incur the EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead if the customisation is declined?

Consider the calculation 40,000 - 22,000 - 12,000 - 5,000. Which figure within it is incurred in either case?

# Hints

Hint 1: Pose a single question to each of the three costs in turn: would this amount differ next month were the firm to decline the engagement?

Hint 2: The office and management overhead is incurred in either case, so acceptance does not create it. The licence is the converse: it is acquired only because the engagement was accepted, although it is a one-off fixed amount.

Hint 3: Take the price, deduct only the costs actually altered by the decision, and exclude the allocated overhead from the calculation entirely.

The final calculation remains yours to perform.
