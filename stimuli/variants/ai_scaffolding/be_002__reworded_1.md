# Explanation

Let's come back to break-even volume, this time for another business. Any business that sells something has a line: below it the business loses money, above it the business earns money. You need three figures, and you have to keep them apart: the costs that stay put no matter what, the money a single sale brings in, and the cost that one more sale adds. Rent, a licence and insurance go in the first bucket; ingredients and packaging go in the third.

What separates the price from the variable cost is the contribution margin, and it is the only piece of a sale that can pay for anything else. If a meal sells for EUR 12 and uses EUR 4.50 of ingredients, it does not put EUR 12 towards the rent. It puts EUR 7.50, since the remaining EUR 4.50 has already gone into the meal.

Divide the fixed costs by that margin and you get the volume at which they are covered exactly:

Break-even quantity = Fixed costs / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A bike repair shop spends EUR 2,400 a month on its workshop, tools and insurance. It charges EUR 50 for a standard service, which takes EUR 20 of parts and consumables. Contribution margin = 50 - 20 = EUR 30 per service. Break-even = 2,400 / 30 = 80 services a month.

The error that hurts businesses most is dividing by the price. For the repair shop that would mean 2,400 / 50 = 48 services, and a shop doing 48 services a month would come nowhere close to paying for its workshop, because the EUR 20 of parts on every job has silently disappeared. Subtract first, divide second, and never swap them. Hold on to that order while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what you divided by.

# Problem

A food truck pays EUR 3,600 a month for its pitch, licence and insurance. A meal is sold at EUR 12 and costs EUR 4.50 in ingredients and packaging. How many meals must it sell each month to break even? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Which of the problem's three numbers does the truck pay whether it sells a single meal or a thousand?

Once the meal itself is paid for, how much of the EUR 12 is really left over for the pitch fee?

Imagine someone divides 3,600 by 12. Where did the EUR 4.50 of ingredients go in that sum?

# Hints

Hint 1: Three numbers count here, and each one has its own job: what the truck pays regardless of sales, what one meal brings in, and what one meal costs to make. Label them before you calculate.

Hint 2: Only some of the EUR 12 is available for the pitch and the licence, because EUR 4.50 of it already went into the meal. Work out what one sale leaves over.

Hint 3: Divide the monthly fixed costs by that leftover amount. That gives the number of meals that covers them exactly.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
