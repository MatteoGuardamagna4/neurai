# Explanation

Let us return to target profit after tax, applied to a different business. A profit target becomes a sales target only once taxation has been taken into account. In the absence of tax the calculation is the break-even calculation with an additional term: the sales required are the fixed costs plus the desired profit, divided by the margin on a single sale. Each sale contributes its margin first to the fixed costs and, once these are covered, to profit.

Taxation determines which profit figure belongs in that numerator. The tax authority takes its share of the profit the business earns, so the amount retained by the owner is smaller than the amount the business generated. A target expressed as retained profit must therefore be converted upwards into the pre-tax profit that yields it, and the conversion is a division by one minus the tax rate, never a multiplication.

With the conversion incorporated:

Quantity = (Fixed costs + After-tax target / (1 - Tax rate)) / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A pottery studio incurs fixed costs of EUR 30,000 per year. A workshop place sells for EUR 120 and costs EUR 45 in clay, glaze and firing. The studio pays tax at 25% and its owner requires EUR 15,000 of profit after tax. Pre-tax profit required = 15,000 / 0.75 = EUR 20,000. Margin = 120 - 45 = EUR 75. Places = (30,000 + 20,000) / 75 = 666.67, hence 667 places.

The characteristic error is to insert the retained figure directly into the numerator. For the pottery studio this would give (30,000 + 15,000) / 75 = 600 places; at 600 places the pre-tax profit is precisely EUR 15,000, one quarter of which is paid in tax, leaving the owner EUR 11,250 instead of the EUR 15,000 required. The target must be grossed up first, and only then treated as though it were an additional fixed cost. Bear this first step in mind as you attempt the problem below; should your answer be incorrect, my first question will concern your treatment of the tax rate.

# Problem

A training studio has fixed costs of EUR 96,000 a year. A course sells for EUR 400 and costs EUR 160 to run. The studio pays tax at 20% on its profits and wants to earn EUR 48,000 after tax. How many courses must it sell? Please attempt it yourself first. I shall not disclose the answer: if your result is incorrect, I shall ask a question about your working and provide a hint, after which you may try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before commenting on your result, I would like to ask three questions.

What profit must the studio earn before tax in order for EUR 48,000 to remain after the tax liability?

Did the tax rate enter your calculation as a multiplication or as a division, and what indicates which is correct?

Consider the calculation (96,000 + 48,000) / 240. Who takes a share of the profit under that plan, and how much remains?

# Hints

Hint 1: Tax is levied on the profit the studio earns, so the figure that determines sales volume is the profit before tax, not the amount ultimately retained by the owner.

Hint 2: If the tax authority takes one fifth of the pre-tax profit, the owner retains four fifths of it. Recover the pre-tax figure by dividing the target by one minus the tax rate.

Hint 3: Treat that pre-tax profit as though it were an additional fixed cost: add it to the EUR 96,000 and divide the total by the margin on one course.

The final calculation remains yours to perform.
