# Explanation

Let's figure out a special order decision together. It is a one-off piece of business a company can take or turn down, and deciding well means putting a single question to every cost on the page: does taking the order change it? Costs that move with the decision are relevant to it. Costs that will be paid whatever the company decides are not, no matter how carefully the costing system spreads them over individual units.

Allocated fixed overhead is the usual trap. The accounts spread rent, salaries and depreciation across the units produced so each carries a share, but the rent does not go up because one more order was accepted. The opposite case matters just as much: a cost that only exists because of the order, such as a special die or an extra shift, is fully relevant even though the accounts list it under fixed costs.

For an order that can be made with spare capacity:

Incremental profit = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost) - Incremental fixed costs

Worked example. A print shop with idle presses is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink; the shop also allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster, and the job needs a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. The allocated EUR 2 is paid whether or not the order is taken, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 2,000 x (7 - 4) - 1,500 = 6,000 - 1,500 = EUR 4,500. Charging the allocated overhead would have shown 2,000 x 1 - 1,500 = EUR 500 and made a good order look marginal.

The slip is comparing the offered price with the full unit cost, variable plus allocated overhead. At the print shop that sets EUR 7 against a EUR 6 cost and makes the job look barely worth doing; had the allocated share been EUR 4, the shop would have turned the order down and finished the month EUR 4,500 worse off, with idle presses and the same overhead to pay. Judge the order on the costs it changes. Hold on to that test while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking which costs you included.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Which of the three costs in the problem would change if the manufacturer said no to this order?

What happens to the EUR 10 of allocated overhead if the order is turned down: does the company stop paying it?

Imagine someone works out 5,000 x (34 - 22 - 10) - 15,000. Which cost in that expression is the same with or without the order?

# Hints

Hint 1: A cost belongs here only if taking the order changes it. Go through the three costs you were given and mark each one as changed or unchanged by the decision.

Hint 2: The allocated overhead is unchanged: the same total is paid either way, and spreading it over more units does not create a new cost. The tooling charge is different, since it only exists if the order is taken.

Hint 3: Multiply the units by the contribution margin per unit, price minus variable cost, then subtract the one-off cost the order causes.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
