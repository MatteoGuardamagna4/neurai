# Explanation

Let us examine markup and margin together. They describe the same difference between the cost of a product and its selling price, but they measure that difference against different bases, and confusing them is among the most costly errors in retail pricing. A markup expresses the difference as a percentage of the cost. A gross margin expresses the same difference as a percentage of the selling price. Since the price is always the larger base, the margin percentage is always the smaller figure for any given product.

The practical implication is that a target margin cannot be achieved by adding that percentage to the cost. If a buyer requires two fifths of the selling price to be gross profit, the cost must constitute the remaining three fifths of the price, so the price equals the cost divided by 0.6 rather than the cost multiplied by 1.4. Expressing the relationship as an equation, cost equals price multiplied by one minus the margin, makes the rearrangement evident.

The rule is a division rather than an addition:

Price = Cost / (1 - Target margin)

Worked example. A shop purchases a lamp for EUR 30 and seeks a gross margin of 25% of the selling price. If the margin is one quarter of the price, the cost is the remaining three quarters: price = 30 / 0.75 = EUR 40. Verification: the margin is 40 - 30 = EUR 10, one quarter of 40. Adding 25% to the cost would have yielded EUR 37.50, a margin of only 20%.

The characteristic error is to multiply the cost by one plus the target margin. A lamp priced in this manner sells for EUR 37.50 in the belief that a quarter of the price is gross profit, whereas the profit of EUR 7.50 is only one fifth of it. The discrepancy increases with the target: a margin of half the price requires the cost to be doubled, while adding half to the cost yields a margin of only one third. The two measures are related by margin = markup / (1 + markup).
