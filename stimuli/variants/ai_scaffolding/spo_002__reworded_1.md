# Explanation

Let's come back to a one-off job decision, this time in another business. Such a job should be judged on what it changes, and the costing system is not built to answer that. A cost is relevant to the decision only if taking the work makes it different. Costs that will be paid whatever is decided are irrelevant, however carefully the accounts share them out across jobs, and a share of a cost you cannot avoid is still a cost you cannot avoid.

Allocated overhead is the classic trap, and it catches people in both directions. Office rent and management salaries are spread across jobs so each carries a slice, which is the right way to report a job's full cost, but the rent does not go up when one more job is taken. On the other hand, a licence, a permit or a tool bought only because the job was accepted is fully relevant, even though it is a fixed amount rather than a cost per hour.

For work that fits into capacity the business already has:

Incremental profit = Price - Costs the job causes

Worked example. A photographer with a free weekend is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the job cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking this size, and the couple want a drone permit that costs EUR 150 and is bought only for this wedding. The EUR 400 of studio rent is paid whatever happens that weekend, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 1,800 - 700 - 150 = EUR 950. Charging the allocated rent would have shown EUR 550 and made a very good booking look ordinary.

The slip is comparing the offered price with the full cost the accounts report. The photographer would set EUR 1,800 against a full cost of EUR 1,250 and decide the wedding was hardly worth the weekend; had the allocated share been bigger, the booking would have been refused and the free weekend would have earned nothing. Judge the job on the cash it changes, and leave the allocation to its separate job of reporting. Hold on to that test while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking which costs you included.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Which of the three costs in the problem would be any different next month if the firm said no to this job?

Does the firm stop paying the EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead if it turns the customisation down?

Imagine someone works out 40,000 - 22,000 - 12,000 - 5,000. Which figure in that line gets paid either way?

# Hints

Hint 1: Put one question to each of the three costs in turn: would this amount be any different next month if the firm turned the job down?

Hint 2: The office and management overhead is paid either way, so taking the job does not create it. The licence is the opposite: it is bought only because the job was taken, even though it is a one-off fixed amount.

Hint 3: Take the price, subtract only the costs the decision really changes, and leave the allocated overhead out completely.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
