# Explanation

Let us examine price elasticity together. Every pricing decision depends on an assumption about how customers will respond, and elasticity expresses that assumption as a number. It compares the movement in quantity with the movement in price, and the comparison is valid only in percentage terms. Raw amounts cannot meaningfully be divided by one another: cups per euro is not a property of demand, since identical demand would yield an entirely different figure were the price quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured relative to the starting position. If sales decline from 1,000 to 900, the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price increases from EUR 4 to EUR 5, the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of these two percentages is the elasticity, which is conventionally reported as a positive number although price and quantity move in opposite directions.

Formally:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery increases the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales decline from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unaltered.

The characteristic error is to divide the absolute change in quantity by the absolute change in price. For the bakery this would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure devoid of meaning outside that particular pair of units. A second, subtler variant measures both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, producing a different result for a price increase than for the identical reduction reversed. The starting value is always the base. Bear this in mind as you attempt the problem below; should your answer be incorrect, my first question will concern what you divided by what.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Please attempt it yourself first. I shall not disclose the answer: if your result is incorrect, I shall ask a question about your working and provide a hint, after which you may try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before commenting on your result, I would like to ask three questions.

Expressed as a percentage, what proportion of the original 1,000 cups was lost?

Expressed as a percentage, what proportion of the original EUR 4.00 was added to the price?

Consider dividing 200 cups by EUR 1. What would that result become were the price quoted in cents?

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity compares two percentage changes, never two absolute amounts: cups and euros cannot meaningfully be divided by one another.

Hint 2: Calculate each percentage separately relative to its starting value: the proportion of the original 1,000 cups lost, and the proportion added to the original EUR 4.00.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price. A result below one indicates that demand responds less than proportionately.
