# Explanation

Let's come back to price elasticity, this time for another business. A price rise that works well for one business ruins another, depending on how strongly customers react. Elasticity measures that reaction as the ratio of two percentage changes: how far quantity moved against how far price moved. Working in percentages is not a matter of style but the only way the number can mean anything, since it removes the units and leaves a clean comparison of responsiveness.

Each percentage is taken against its own starting value. Members lost are a share of the members there were before, and the price rise is a share of the price there was before. You read the result against one: below one, demand is inelastic and total revenue rises with the price; above one, it is elastic and revenue falls; at exactly one, the two effects cancel out.

Written out:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A cinema raises its ticket price from EUR 10 to EUR 11 and weekly admissions drop from 2,000 to 1,800. Admissions fall by 200 / 2,000 = 10%. The price rises by 1 / 10 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, the unit-elastic case where revenue does not change.

The slip is dividing raw members by raw euros. For the cinema that gives 200 admissions per euro, which would turn into 2 admissions per cent for exactly the same behaviour, so it cannot be describing the customers at all. A second version measures the changes against the end values instead of the starting ones, which makes a price rise and the identical price cut give different elasticities for the same two points. Hold on to that comparison with one while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what your two percentages were.

# Problem

A gym raises its monthly membership from EUR 20 to EUR 24 and the number of members falls from 1,000 to 700. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

What percentage of its original 1,000 members did the gym lose?

What percentage was added to the original EUR 20 price?

Imagine someone reports 75 members per euro. Is that a fact about the gym's customers, or about the currency the price is quoted in?

# Hints

Hint 1: The answer must be a ratio of two percentages. Members and euros are different units, so dividing one by the other gives a number with no meaning.

Hint 2: Take each change as a share of where it began: the lost members as a share of the original 1,000, and the price rise as a share of the original EUR 20.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage fall in members by the percentage rise in price. Above one means demand is elastic and the gym is losing revenue.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
