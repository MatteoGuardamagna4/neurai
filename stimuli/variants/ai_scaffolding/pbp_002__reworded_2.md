# Explanation

Let us return to the payback period, applied to a different investment. It is the first question a prudent owner poses: how long until the funds are recovered? It is the least sophisticated of the appraisal measures and among the most frequently used, since it is rapid, readily explained and addresses risk directly: the sooner the funds return, the shorter the period of exposure. Its limitation is that it conveys nothing about subsequent events, so a project that pays back quickly and then ceases appears identical to one that pays back quickly and continues for a decade.

The measure is based on cash, and this is where it is most frequently misapplied. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is an accounting convention that allocates an asset's cost over its life rather than a payment made by anyone. The funds for the asset left the business once, at the outset, and they already appear in the numerator awaiting recovery.

With a constant annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry acquires a EUR 45,000 machine that generates EUR 15,000 of cash per year. Depreciated over five years, it bears a charge of EUR 9,000 per year, so the accounts report a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, a considerable overstatement, since the depreciation has been deducted from a return that never actually bore it.

The characteristic error is to divide by the reported profit. This charges the asset twice, once as the full price in the numerator and again as depreciation deducted from the annual return, and the result is excessively pessimistic. Where cash flows are uneven, the division ceases to apply altogether: cumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally exceeded. Bear this in mind as you attempt the problem below; should your answer be incorrect, my first question will concern which of the two annual figures you used.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? Please attempt it yourself first. I shall not disclose the answer: if your result is incorrect, I shall ask a question about your working and provide a hint, after which you may try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before commenting on your result, I would like to ask three questions.

Of the EUR 24,000 and the EUR 14,000, which represents money actually received in the hotel's bank account?

Does the EUR 10,000 of depreciation leave the hotel each year, or did those funds leave once when the kitchen was acquired?

Consider dividing 84,000 by 14,000. Which cost is counted twice in that calculation?

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback concerns how long the funds invested take to be recovered, so both parts of the division must be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures describe the same year. One is the cash generated by the kitchen; the other is the accounting result after a depreciation charge that no one actually pays.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow and express the result in years.

The final calculation remains yours to perform.
