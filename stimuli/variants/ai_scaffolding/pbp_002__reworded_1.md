# Explanation

Let's come back to the payback period, this time for another investment. It is the first thing a careful owner wants to know: how long until the money comes back? It is the roughest of the appraisal measures and one of the most used, because it is quick, easy to explain and speaks straight to risk: the sooner the money comes back, the less time the business is exposed. Its weakness is that it says nothing about what comes after, so a project that pays back fast and then stops looks the same as one that pays back fast and runs for ten years.

The measure is built on cash, and that is where it usually goes wrong. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been taken out of it, and depreciation is an accounting tool that spreads an asset's cost over its life, not a payment anyone makes. The money for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it already sits in the top of the fraction waiting to be recovered.

With the same cash flow every year:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry buys a EUR 45,000 machine that brings in EUR 15,000 of cash a year. Depreciated over five years, it carries a charge of EUR 9,000 a year, so the accounts show a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, much too long, because depreciation has been taken out of a return that never actually paid it.

The slip is dividing by the reported profit. That charges the asset twice, once as the full price on top of the fraction and again as depreciation taken out of the yearly return, and the answer ends up far too gloomy. When cash flows are uneven, the division stops working altogether: add up the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally passed. Hold on to that while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking which of the two yearly figures you used.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Of the EUR 24,000 and the EUR 14,000, which one is money that actually lands in the hotel's bank account?

Does the EUR 10,000 of depreciation leave the hotel every year, or did that money leave once when the kitchen was bought?

Imagine someone divides 84,000 by 14,000. Which cost has been counted twice in that sum?

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the money put in takes to come back, so both parts of the division have to be in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures describe the same year. One is the cash the kitchen brings in; the other is what the accounts show after a depreciation charge that nobody actually pays.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the yearly cash flow and give the answer in years.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
