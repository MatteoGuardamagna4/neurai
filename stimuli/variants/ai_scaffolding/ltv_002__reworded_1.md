# Explanation

Let's come back to customer lifetime value, this time for another business. A subscription company does not make its money on the first sale, so that first payment badly undervalues a customer. Lifetime value is what one customer is worth over the whole relationship, and it is the number that tells you how much it makes sense to spend winning them. It turns a marketing budget from an expense into an investment whose return you can check.

It is made of two pieces, and both are easy to get slightly wrong. The first is what the company keeps each period, a margin rather than a price: a EUR 80 subscription that costs EUR 32 to fulfil leaves EUR 48, and only that EUR 48 can cover acquisition, overheads and profit. The second is how long the customer stays, which comes from churn: when a steady share leaves each month, the average life is one divided by that share, so 5% monthly churn means twenty months and 10% means ten.

Put them together:

Lifetime value = Period revenue x Gross margin / Churn rate for that period

Worked example. A magazine charges EUR 15 a month. Printing, postage and editorial take 20% of that, leaving an 80% gross margin, and 4% of subscribers cancel every month. Monthly margin = 15 x 0.8 = EUR 12. The average subscriber stays 1 / 0.04 = 25 months. Lifetime value = 12 x 25 = EUR 300.

The slip is building the figure on revenue. For the magazine that gives 15 / 0.04 = EUR 375 rather than EUR 300, and the gap grows as margins shrink: a business keeping just a third of its revenue would overvalue every customer three times over. Another form of the slip assumes everyone stays exactly a year, which discards precisely what churn tells you and hides any gain from better retention. Hold on to that while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what you did with the gross margin.

# Problem

A meal-kit company charges EUR 80 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 60% of that revenue, and 8% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Out of the EUR 80 a customer pays every month, how much does this company really keep?

If 8% of customers cancel each month, how many months does the average customer stay, and where does that figure come from?

Imagine someone divides 80 by 0.08. Which cost is still sitting inside that figure?

# Hints

Hint 1: Lifetime value has two parts: what the company keeps from a customer each month, and how many months that customer stays. Work them out one at a time.

Hint 2: The company does not keep all EUR 80. Apply the gross margin first, so the cost of the ingredients and delivery is already taken out.

Hint 3: A monthly cancellation rate of 8% means an average life of one divided by 0.08. Multiplying by that life or dividing by the churn rate gives the same answer.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
