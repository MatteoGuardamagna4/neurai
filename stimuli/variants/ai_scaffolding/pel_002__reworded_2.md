# Explanation

Let us return to price elasticity, applied to a different business. A price increase that succeeds for one business may prove ruinous for another, depending on the strength of the customer response. Elasticity measures that response as the ratio of two percentage changes: the movement in quantity relative to the movement in price. The use of percentages is not a matter of presentation but the only means by which the figure acquires meaning, since it removes the units and leaves a pure comparison of responsiveness.

Each percentage is calculated against its own starting value. Members lost are expressed as a share of the members previously held, and the price increase as a share of the previous price. The result is interpreted relative to one: below one, demand is inelastic and total revenue rises with the price; above one, it is elastic and revenue falls; at exactly one, the two effects offset each other.

Formally:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A cinema increases its ticket price from EUR 10 to EUR 11 and weekly admissions decline from 2,000 to 1,800. Admissions fall by 200 / 2,000 = 10%. The price rises by 1 / 10 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, the unit-elastic case in which revenue is unchanged.

The characteristic error is to divide absolute members by absolute euros. For the cinema this yields 200 admissions per euro, which would become 2 admissions per cent for identical behaviour, so it cannot describe the customers at all. A second variant measures the changes against the final values rather than the initial ones, so that a price increase and the identical reduction yield different elasticities for the same pair of points. Bear the comparison with one in mind as you attempt the problem below; should your answer be incorrect, my first question will concern your two percentages.

# Problem

A gym raises its monthly membership from EUR 20 to EUR 24 and the number of members falls from 1,000 to 700. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? # Diagnostic questions

Before commenting on your result, I would like to ask three questions.

What percentage of its original 1,000 members did the gym lose?

What percentage was added to the original EUR 20 price?

Consider a reported figure of 75 members per euro. Does it describe the gym's customers, or the currency in which the price is quoted?

# Hints

Hint 1: The result must be a ratio of two percentages. Members and euros are different units, so dividing one by the other produces a figure without meaning.

Hint 2: Express each change as a share of its starting value: the members lost as a share of the original 1,000, and the price increase as a share of the original EUR 20.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage decline in members by the percentage increase in price. A value above one indicates elastic demand and a loss of revenue for the gym.
