# Explanation

Let's come back to markup and margin, this time for another shop. One and the same euro of profit can be called a 25% markup or a 20% margin. Both describe the gap between what an item costs and what it sells for. A markup measures that gap against the cost; a gross margin measures it against the selling price. Because the price is the larger base, the margin percentage is always the smaller number for the very same item.

So in practice you cannot reach a target margin by adding that percentage to the cost. If three tenths of the price is meant to be gross profit, the other seven tenths must be the cost, so the price is the cost divided by 0.7. Writing it as cost equals price times one minus the margin makes the rearranging obvious and takes away the urge to multiply.

The rule is a division:

Price = Cost / (1 - Target margin)

Worked example. A bookshop pays EUR 45 for a hardback and wants a gross margin of 40% of the selling price. If the margin takes 40% of the price, the cost is the other 60%: price = 45 / 0.6 = EUR 75. Check: the profit is 75 - 45 = EUR 30, which is 40% of 75. Adding 40% to the cost would have given EUR 63, where the EUR 18 of profit is only 28.6% of the price.

The slip is multiplying the cost by one plus the target margin. The bookshop would then charge EUR 63 and think it earned 40% of the price, when the EUR 18 of profit is 28.6% of it. The gap widens as the target rises, which is why the error does most damage on the items a shop leans on most. The two measures are linked by margin = markup / (1 + markup), a conversion worth learning by heart. Hold on to that while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what you applied the 30% to.

# Problem

A boutique buys a jacket for EUR 84 and wants a gross margin of 30% of the selling price. What price should it set? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

When you used the 30%, did it go onto the EUR 84 the boutique paid or onto the price it will charge?

If three tenths of the selling price is gross profit, what fraction of the selling price is the cost?

Imagine the jacket is priced at EUR 109.20. Divide the profit by that price: what margin does the boutique really earn?

# Hints

Hint 1: First decide what the 30% is a percentage of. A gross margin is always measured against the selling price, never against what the boutique paid.

Hint 2: If three tenths of the price is to be gross profit, then seven tenths of it is the EUR 84 the jacket cost. Write that as an equation before you rearrange it.

Hint 3: Divide the cost by one minus the target margin, then check by dividing the profit by the price you set.
