# Explanation

Let's come back to return on investment, this time for another purchase. A machine that cost EUR 100,000 and produced EUR 160,000 has not returned 160%. A return measures what was earned beyond the money put in, per euro put in. The cash a project generates usually includes the original outlay coming back, and handing someone their own money back is not a profit, so that part has to come out before you divide anything.

Once the gain is isolated, the bottom of the fraction is the amount that was at risk. This is the second place the calculation goes wrong: dividing the gain by the cash generated instead of by the cash invested gives a smaller and just as wrong answer, because it measures the profit against the proceeds rather than against the exposure.

Written out:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer, and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the money spent.

The slip is the money multiple: total returned divided by amount invested. For the haulier that shows 120% on a trailer that really returned 20%, and the error always flatters, which is why it lives on in pitch decks. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project that returns exactly what it cost has earned nothing, so the right method gives zero and the multiple gives one. Hold on to the break-even test while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking what happened to the original EUR 100,000.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

How much of the EUR 160,000 the equipment produced is profit, and how much is the purchase price coming back?

What did you divide the gain by, and why is that the right base for a return?

Imagine someone reports 160%. What would the same method say about equipment that produced exactly EUR 100,000?

# Hints

Hint 1: The EUR 160,000 is everything the equipment produced, and part of it is just the EUR 100,000 coming back. Find the gain first.

Hint 2: The bottom of a return is always the money put at risk, not the money that came out at the end.

Hint 3: Divide the gain by the EUR 100,000 invested and give it as a percentage.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
