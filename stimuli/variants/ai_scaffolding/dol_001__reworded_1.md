# Explanation

Let's figure out operating leverage together. Picture two companies with identical sales and profit: hit them both with a bad quarter and one barely notices while the other is badly hurt. It comes down to how much of each cost base is fixed. When a firm's costs rise and fall with sales, a dip in sales costs it little, because its costs drop as well. When a firm carries heavy fixed costs, it keeps paying them regardless, so the very same dip bites much harder into profit.

The degree of operating leverage turns that sensitivity into a number. It sets what all the sales contribute against what is left once the fixed costs are paid, and you read it as a multiplier: a figure of 3 means each 1% change in sales shifts operating profit by 3%, up in a good year and down in a bad one. For a profitable firm it is always above one, since contribution is always bigger than the profit left after fixed costs.

Here is the ratio:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A workshop sells 5,000 units at EUR 40 each, with a variable cost of EUR 24, and its fixed costs are EUR 40,000. Contribution = 5,000 x 16 = EUR 80,000. Operating profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = EUR 40,000. Degree of operating leverage = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00, so sales up 10% means profit up 20%.

The usual slip is flipping the ratio. Profit divided by contribution gives a number under one, which cannot multiply anything; it actually tells you what share of contribution is left after the fixed costs. The check is easy: leverage is about amplification, so an answer under one means you divided the wrong way. Hold on to that check while you tackle the problem below, because if your result is off, I will start by asking which figure you put on top.

# Problem

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units a year at EUR 50 each, with a variable cost of EUR 30 per unit and fixed costs of EUR 120,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? Have a go on your own first. I won't give you the answer: if you get it wrong, I'll ask you something about how you worked it out and offer a hint, and then you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I comment on your result, here are three questions for you.

Before the fixed costs come out, what do all 10,000 units contribute in total?

Which of your two figures is bigger, and which one has to go on top for the answer to be a multiplier?

Imagine someone reports 0.40. Can a number under one describe how much profit gets amplified when sales move?

# Hints

Hint 1: Operating leverage compares two amounts the business already knows: what all its sales contribute, and what remains of that after the fixed costs. Find both before you build any ratio.

Hint 2: Total contribution is units sold times the margin on each one. Operating profit is that contribution minus the fixed costs, so it is always the smaller figure.

Hint 3: The degree of operating leverage is contribution divided by operating profit, the bigger over the smaller, so for a profitable firm the answer is always above one.

The last step of the calculation is for you to do.
