# Explanation

Let's look at operating leverage together. Two companies with the same sales and profit can react completely differently to a bad quarter. The difference is how much of their cost base is fixed. A firm whose costs move with sales loses little when sales dip, because its costs fall too. A firm carrying heavy fixed costs keeps paying them whatever happens, so the same dip in sales cuts far deeper into profit.

The degree of operating leverage puts a number on that sensitivity. It compares what all the sales contribute with what survives after the fixed costs, and it reads as a multiplier: a figure of 3 means every 1% change in sales moves operating profit by 3%, upwards in a good year and downwards in a bad one. It is always greater than one for a profitable firm, because contribution is always larger than the profit left after fixed costs.

The ratio is:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A workshop sells 5,000 units at EUR 40 with a variable cost of EUR 24, and has fixed costs of EUR 40,000. Contribution = 5,000 x 16 = EUR 80,000. Operating profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = EUR 40,000. Degree of operating leverage = 80,000 / 40,000 = 2.00, so a 10% rise in sales lifts profit by 20%.

The mistake is to write the ratio upside down. Dividing profit by contribution gives a number below one, which cannot be a multiplier of anything and in fact reports the share of contribution that survives the fixed costs. The check is quick: leverage measures amplification, so if your answer is less than one you have divided the wrong way round. Keep that check in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because writing the ratio upside down is the usual error.

# Problem

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units a year at EUR 50 each, with a variable cost of EUR 30 per unit and fixed costs of EUR 120,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin on a unit is 50 - 30 = EUR 20, so total contribution is 10,000 x 20 = EUR 200,000. Step 2: operating profit is 200,000 - 120,000 = EUR 80,000. Step 3: the degree of operating leverage is 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.50. It reads as a multiplier: a 10% rise in sales would lift operating profit by 25%, and a 10% fall would cut it by the same. Dividing the other way round would give 0.40, which is the share of contribution that survives the fixed costs, a different and much less useful number. Notice how to read the result: it is not a percentage but a multiplier, so a 10% swing in sales becomes a 25% swing in profit, in whichever direction the sales move.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: compute total contribution as units times the margin on one, subtract the fixed costs to get operating profit, then divide contribution by profit. Multiply the result by any percentage change in sales to get the change in profit. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
