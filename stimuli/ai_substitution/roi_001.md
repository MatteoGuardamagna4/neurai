# Explanation

Let's look at return on investment together. It is the most quoted figure in business and the most often miscalculated, almost always in the same direction. It answers one question: for every euro committed, how much came back on top of that euro? The phrase "on top of" is the whole difficulty, because the money a project returns usually includes the original stake, and the stake was never profit.

So the calculation has two steps that must not be merged. First separate the gain from the stake: if EUR 400,000 goes in and EUR 500,000 comes out, the gain is EUR 100,000 and the rest is the company's own money returning. Then divide that gain by what was put at risk, because the denominator of any return is the amount committed and not the amount received.

In symbols:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A shop spends EUR 50,000 refitting itself and the refit brings in EUR 65,000 of extra gross profit over its life. The gain is 65,000 - 50,000 = EUR 15,000. ROI = 15,000 / 50,000 = 0.3, a return of 30% on the money invested.

The mistake is to divide the total returned by the amount invested and call it a return. That is the money multiple: it counts the stake as profit and turns a respectable project into a spectacular one on paper. A quieter version divides the gain by the total received rather than by the investment, which understates the return instead. A useful check is that a break-even project must show a return of zero, not of one. Keep that check in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because the stake is what people most often count twice.

# Problem

A company invests EUR 400,000 in a project and receives EUR 500,000 back in total over the project's life. What is the return on investment? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: separate the stake from the gain. Of the EUR 500,000 received, EUR 400,000 is the company's own money coming home, so the gain is 500,000 - 400,000 = EUR 100,000. Step 2: divide the gain by what was invested: 100,000 / 400,000 = 0.25, a return of 25%. Reporting 500,000 / 400,000 = 125% would describe the same project as five times better than it is, because it counts the returned stake as profit. Dividing by the EUR 500,000 received instead would give 20%, which measures the gain as a share of the proceeds rather than of the risk taken. Notice the check worth applying every time: a project that returns exactly its cost gives a return of zero, so any method that reports 100% for that case is the wrong method.

Use these two steps for the rest of the unit: subtract the investment from the total returned to isolate the gain, then divide that gain by the investment. If the question spans several years and asks for an annual figure, divide once more by the number of years. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
