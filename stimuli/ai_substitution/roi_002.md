# Explanation

Let's look at return on investment again, on a different purchase. A machine costing EUR 100,000 that produced EUR 160,000 has not returned 160%. A return measures what was earned beyond the money committed, per euro committed. The cash a project throws off normally contains the original outlay coming home, and returning someone's own money to them is not a profit, so that part has to be taken out before any division happens.

Once the gain is isolated, the denominator is the amount that was at risk. This is the second place the calculation slips: dividing the gain by the cash generated rather than by the cash invested gives a smaller and equally wrong answer, because it measures the profit against the proceeds instead of against the exposure.

Written out:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the money spent.

The mistake is the money multiple: total returned over amount invested. For the haulier above that reports 120% on a trailer that actually returned 20%, and the error is always flattering, which is why it survives in pitch decks. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project returning exactly what it cost has earned nothing, so the right method gives zero and the multiple gives one. Keep the break-even test in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because counting the stake as profit is the usual error.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the gain is 160,000 - 100,000 = EUR 60,000, because the first EUR 100,000 of the cash is the company's own money returning. Step 2: divide by what was invested: 60,000 / 100,000 = 0.6, a return of 60% over the life of the equipment. Reporting 160,000 / 100,000 = 160% would count the returned stake as profit and flatter the purchase badly; dividing the gain by the EUR 160,000 generated would give 37.5% and measure it against the wrong base. Notice that the two wrong answers err in opposite directions: the multiple flatters the purchase, while dividing by the cash generated understates it.

Use these two steps for the rest of the unit: subtract the investment from the total returned, then divide by the investment. To turn a required return into a target, multiply the investment by one plus that return, since the stake has to come back as well. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
