# Explanation

Let's look at operating leverage again, in a different industry. Software firms swing from losses to large profits on modest changes in sales, for arithmetic reasons. Almost all of their cost is fixed, spent before a single copy is sold, while each extra licence costs very little to deliver. That combination makes profit extremely sensitive to volume: the fixed costs must be paid whatever happens, so every sale beyond the break-even point drops almost entirely into profit, and every sale lost takes almost the whole margin with it.

The degree of operating leverage measures that sensitivity as a multiplier. It is total contribution divided by operating profit, and because contribution is the larger of the two it always exceeds one for a profitable firm. A value of 4 says that a 5% fall in sales would cut operating profit by 20%, which is why a business with high leverage needs a bigger margin of safety than one without.

In symbols:

Degree of operating leverage = Total contribution / Operating profit

Worked example. A consultancy bills 600 days at EUR 900 a day. Each billed day costs EUR 300 in contractor fees, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 240,000. Contribution = 600 x 600 = EUR 360,000. Operating profit = 360,000 - 240,000 = EUR 120,000. Leverage = 360,000 / 120,000 = 3.00: a 10% swing in billings moves profit by 30%.

The mistake is to invert the ratio. Operating profit divided by contribution always lands below one and cannot amplify anything; it reports the fraction of contribution that outlives the fixed costs, which is a perfectly real number answering a different question. If the result of a leverage calculation is less than one, the two figures have been swapped. Keep that in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because inverting the ratio is the usual error.

# Problem

A software firm sells 4,000 licences a year at EUR 250 each. Each licence costs EUR 50 in support and hosting, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 550,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin on a licence is 250 - 50 = EUR 200, so contribution is 4,000 x 200 = EUR 800,000. Step 2: operating profit is 800,000 - 550,000 = EUR 250,000. Step 3: leverage is 800,000 / 250,000 = 3.20. A 10% rise in licence sales would raise operating profit by 32%; a 10% fall would cut it by as much, which is exactly why software firms are so sensitive to a bad quarter. Inverting the ratio would give 0.3125, the share of contribution left after fixed costs, which answers a different question. Notice what the figure warns about: with leverage above three, losing a sixth of the licence sales would wipe out half the operating profit.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: multiply the volume by the margin on one unit to get contribution, subtract the fixed costs for operating profit, then divide contribution by profit. Apply any percentage change in sales by multiplying it by that figure. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
