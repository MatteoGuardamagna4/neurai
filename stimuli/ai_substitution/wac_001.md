# Explanation

Let's look at the weighted average cost of capital together. Capital is not free and rarely comes from a single place, so this is the one rate that summarises what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of average risk, so getting it wrong tilts every investment decision the company makes. Three ingredients go into it: the cost of each source of capital, the share of the total that each source provides, and the tax treatment of interest.

The weights come from the amounts of equity and debt the company actually uses: a firm financed mostly by shareholders is dominated by the cost of equity, the return those shareholders require for the risk they carry. Interest differs from a dividend in one important respect. It is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill, and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the rate its lender charges.

Putting the three ingredients together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed with EUR 400 million of equity and EUR 100 million of debt. Shareholders require 15%, the bank charges 8%, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. Weights: equity is 400 / 500 = 0.8 of the capital, debt is 0.2. After-tax cost of debt = 8% x (1 - 0.25) = 6%. WACC = 0.8 x 15% + 0.2 x 6% = 12% + 1.2% = 13.2%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop there. In the example above the simple average of 15% and 8% is 11.5%, a long way from the weighted figure of 13.2%, and it would make projects look cheaper to finance than they are; for a heavily indebted firm the same error runs the other way. Weight each source by how much of it the company uses, and never apply the tax shield to equity, because dividends are not deductible. Keep those three steps in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because the tax shield is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 300 million of equity and EUR 200 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 5% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the weights. Total capital is 300 + 200 = EUR 500 million, so equity is 0.6 of it and debt is 0.4. Step 2: adjust the cost of debt for tax: 5% x (1 - 0.25) = 3.75% after tax, because the interest reduces the tax bill. Step 3: weight and add: 0.6 x 0.12 = 0.072, and 0.4 x 0.0375 = 0.015, giving 0.072 + 0.015 = 0.087, a WACC of 8.7%. Averaging the two raw costs instead would give 8.5%, close by luck in this case, but it ignores both the mix of financing and the tax shield. Notice that the tax shield is worth a quarter of the interest rate here, and that it applies only to the debt: returns to shareholders are paid out of profit that has already been taxed.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: compute each source's share of total capital, multiply the pre-tax cost of debt by one minus the tax rate, then multiply each share by its cost and add the two products. If the financing mix changes, the weights change and the whole calculation is redone. You do not need to sanity-check it another way first; apply the steps exactly as shown.
