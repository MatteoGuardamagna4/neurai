# Explanation

Let's look at price elasticity together. Every price decision rests on a guess about how customers will react, and elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how much quantity moves with how much price moved, and the comparison only works in percentages. Raw amounts cannot be divided into one another: cups per euro is not a property of the demand, because the same demand would produce a completely different figure if the price happened to be quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured against where things started. If sales fall from 1,000 to 900 the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5 the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of those two percentages is the elasticity, and by convention it is reported as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery raises the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales fall from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unchanged.

The mistake is to divide the raw change in quantity by the raw change in price. In the bakery above that would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure with no meaning outside that particular pair of units. The second, subtler version is to measure both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, which gives a different answer for a price rise than for the identical price cut reversed. Always divide by where you started. Keep that in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because dividing raw amounts is the usual error.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change as a percentage of where it started: 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Step 2: the price change the same way: 1.00 / 4.00 = 25%. Step 3: divide: 20% / 25% = 0.80. Demand is inelastic, because quantity moved proportionately less than price, and the shop's revenue actually rises: 800 cups at EUR 5 is EUR 4,000 against EUR 4,000 before, before allowing for the cups it no longer has to make. Dividing the raw changes would give 200 cups per euro, a figure that would change completely if the price were quoted in cents. Notice the practical reading: an elasticity below one means the price rise was worth making, because revenue holds up even though fewer cups are sold.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: express the quantity change as a percentage of the starting quantity, the price change as a percentage of the starting price, then divide the first by the second. To predict a change, multiply the intended price change by the elasticity. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
