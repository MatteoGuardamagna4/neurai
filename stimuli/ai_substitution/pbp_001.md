# Explanation

Let's look at the payback period together. It is the crudest of the investment appraisal measures and one of the most widely used: it asks how long the money put into a project takes to come back. A shorter payback means the company is exposed for less time, which is why lenders and cautious boards like the measure, and it is easy to explain to people who would never sit through a discounted cash flow. Its weakness is that it ignores everything that happens after the money has been recovered.

The measure is built entirely on cash. That distinction matters because the profit reported in the accounts is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is a bookkeeping charge that spreads the cost of an asset over its life rather than a payment made to anyone. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it is already sitting in the numerator of the calculation.

With an even annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash a year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts show a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 a year, but no cash leaves the business for that depreciation, so it must not be deducted here.

The mistake is to divide the investment by the accounting profit. That deducts depreciation from the annual return while the full purchase price still stands in the numerator, so the cost of the asset is counted twice and the payback comes out far too long. Uneven cash flows need a different treatment but rest on the same principle: accumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which it is finally recovered. Keep that in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because choosing between the two annual figures is the step people most often get wrong.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: identify the cash. The equipment brings in EUR 24,000 a year. The EUR 15,000 profit figure is that same cash after the EUR 9,000 depreciation charge, and depreciation is not a payment to anyone, so the cash figure is the one to use. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 90,000 / 24,000 = 3.75 years. The company gets its money back three quarters of the way through the fourth year. Using the profit figure instead would give 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years, which understates the investment badly by deducting a cost that never leaves the bank account. The fraction has a plain meaning: three full years bring back EUR 72,000, and the remaining EUR 18,000 arrives three quarters of the way through the fourth year.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: take the annual cash flow, ignoring depreciation and any profit figure built on it, and divide the investment by it. If the cash flows are uneven, accumulate them year by year and add the fraction of the year in which the investment is finally recovered. You do not need to check it another way first; apply the steps exactly as shown.
