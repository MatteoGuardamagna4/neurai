# Explanation

Let's look at the weighted average cost of capital again, on a different firm. A company borrowing at 8% whose shareholders want 12% faces neither figure as its cost of capital. The weighted average cost of capital is the single rate that stands for what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of ordinary risk, so an error in it tilts every investment decision the company takes, in the same direction, for as long as the error survives.

Three ingredients go into it. Each source has its own cost: shareholders require a return for the risk they carry, lenders quote an interest rate. Each source provides a share of the total capital, and those shares are the weights. And interest, unlike a dividend, is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the quoted rate.

Putting the three together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed half by equity and half by debt. Shareholders require 16%, the bank charges 10%, and the tax rate is 20%. After-tax cost of debt = 10% x (1 - 0.20) = 8%. WACC = 0.5 x 16% + 0.5 x 8% = 8% + 4% = 12%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop. The firm in the example above happens to be financed half and half, so the weights are equal and the average looks harmless, but the tax shield still matters: ignoring it would report 13% instead of 12%. In a firm financed mostly one way the weights matter far more than the shield, and a simple average can be wrong by several points in either direction. Never apply the shield to equity: dividends are not deductible. Keep those three steps in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because the tax shield is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 150 million of equity and EUR 50 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 8% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the weights. Total capital is 150 + 50 = EUR 200 million, so equity provides 0.75 of it and debt 0.25. Step 2: the tax shield. An 8% interest rate costs the firm 8% x (1 - 0.25) = 6% after tax. Step 3: weight and add: 0.75 x 0.12 = 0.09, and 0.25 x 0.06 = 0.015, giving 0.09 + 0.015 = 0.105, a WACC of 10.5%. A simple average of 12% and 8% would give 10%, which happens to land nearby here only because the numbers are gentle; it ignores that three quarters of this firm's capital is the expensive kind. Notice how much the weights are doing here: three quarters of this firm's capital carries the 12% cost, which is why the answer sits much closer to 12% than to the after-tax cost of its debt.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: compute each source's share of the total capital, multiply the quoted cost of debt by one minus the tax rate, then multiply each share by its cost and add. If the financing mix changes, recompute the weights and start again. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
