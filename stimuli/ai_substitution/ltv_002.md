# Explanation

Let's look at customer lifetime value again, on a different business. A subscription company does not earn its money at the first sale, so the first payment understates a customer badly. Lifetime value is what one customer is worth across the whole relationship, and it is the figure that decides how much can sensibly be spent to win them. It turns a marketing budget from an expense into an investment with a return that can be checked.

Two ingredients build it, and both are easy to get slightly wrong. The first is what the company keeps each period, which is a margin and not a price: a EUR 80 subscription that costs EUR 32 to fulfil leaves EUR 48, and only that EUR 48 can pay for acquisition, overheads and profit. The second is how long the customer stays, which comes from churn: if a steady share leaves each month, the average life is one divided by that share, so 5% monthly churn implies twenty months and 10% implies ten.

Combining them:

Lifetime value = Period revenue x Gross margin / Churn rate for that period

Worked example. A magazine charges EUR 15 a month. Printing, postage and editorial cost it 20% of that, leaving an 80% gross margin, and 4% of subscribers cancel each month. Monthly margin = 15 x 0.8 = EUR 12. The average subscriber stays 1 / 0.04 = 25 months. Lifetime value = 12 x 25 = EUR 300.

The mistake is to build the figure on revenue. For the magazine above that gives 15 / 0.04 = EUR 375 instead of EUR 300, and the gap widens as margins thin: a business keeping only a third of its revenue would overstate every customer threefold. A second version of the error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away exactly the information churn carries and makes retention improvements invisible. Keep that in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because applying the margin is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A meal-kit company charges EUR 80 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 60% of that revenue, and 8% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the monthly gross margin is 80 x 0.6 = EUR 48, which is what is left after the boxes have been packed and delivered. Step 2: with 8% cancelling each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.08 = 12.5 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 48 x 12.5 = EUR 600, the same as dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate. Using the full EUR 80 of revenue would give EUR 1,000 and would let the company spend on acquisition money that is already committed to ingredients and couriers. Notice that lifetime value responds far more to churn than to price: halving the cancellation rate doubles the answer, while a ten per cent price rise moves it by a tenth.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: multiply the period revenue by the gross margin to get what the company keeps, then divide by the churn rate for that same period. If the question gives an average life instead of a churn rate, multiply by it rather than dividing. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
