# Explanation

Let's look at net present value together. It starts from one idea: a euro received in two years is worth less than a euro received today, because a euro today can be put to work and would have grown by then. Net present value applies that idea to a project. It restates every future cash flow in today's money and compares the total with the amount that has to be paid now. If the total is larger the project earns more than the return the company requires and adds value; if it is smaller it does not.

Restating a future amount in today's money is called discounting. Cash arriving in one year is divided by one plus the required return. Cash arriving in two years has waited twice as long, so it is divided by one plus the required return squared, and later years carry higher powers still. The required return is what the company could earn elsewhere at the same risk, so discounting at that rate asks whether the project beats the alternative.

For a project with two years of cash flows:

NPV = CF1 / (1 + r) + CF2 / (1 + r)^2 - Cost paid today

Worked example. A machine costs EUR 40,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 30,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 30,000 at the end of year 2. The company requires a 25% return. Year 1: 30,000 / 1.25 = EUR 24,000. Year 2: 30,000 / 1.25 squared = 30,000 / 1.5625 = EUR 19,200. Present value of the inflows = EUR 43,200, so the NPV is 43,200 - 40,000 = EUR 3,200 and the machine is worth buying.

The mistake is to add the future cash flows together and subtract the cost, or to discount their sum once. In the machine example the raw sum of EUR 60,000 against a cost of EUR 40,000 suggests a gain of EUR 20,000, more than six times the true figure, and it would look just as attractive if the same cash arrived in year ten instead. Discount each year by its own factor, because the entire point of the calculation is that timing matters. Keep that order in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because the second year's exponent is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A project costs EUR 100,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 72,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 72,000 at the end of year 2. The required return is 20% a year. What is the net present value of the project? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: discount year 1: 72,000 / 1.2 = EUR 60,000. Step 2: discount year 2 by two years of waiting: 72,000 / 1.2 squared = 72,000 / 1.44 = EUR 50,000. Step 3: add the present values: 60,000 + 50,000 = EUR 110,000. Step 4: subtract what is paid today: 110,000 - 100,000 = EUR 10,000. The NPV is positive, so the project earns more than the 20% required and is worth doing. Adding the raw cash flows instead would have given 144,000 - 100,000 = EUR 44,000, which counts money that has not arrived yet at full value. Notice that the two inflows are identical in size but not in value: the first is worth EUR 60,000 today and the second only EUR 50,000.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: discount each year's cash by one plus the rate raised to that year's number, add the present values, then subtract the amount paid today. When the required return changes, recompute from the start rather than adjusting the previous answer, because every factor moves. You do not need to try another route first; apply the steps exactly as shown.
