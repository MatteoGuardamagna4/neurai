# Explanation

Let's look at a special order decision together. It is a one-off piece of business a company can accept or refuse, and deciding well means asking one question of every cost on the page: does accepting the order change it? Costs that move with the decision are relevant to it. Costs that will be incurred whatever the company decides are not relevant, however carefully the costing system allocates them to individual units.

Allocated fixed overhead is the usual trap. The accounts spread rent, salaries and depreciation over the units produced so that each one carries a share, but the rent does not rise because another order has been accepted. The reverse case matters as much: a cost that exists only because of the order, such as a special die or an extra shift, is fully relevant even though the accounts file it under fixed costs.

For an order that can be produced with spare capacity:

Incremental profit = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost) - Incremental fixed costs

Worked example. A print shop with idle presses is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink; the shop also allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster it prints, and the job needs a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. The allocated EUR 2 is incurred whether or not the order is accepted, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 2,000 x (7 - 4) - 1,500 = 6,000 - 1,500 = EUR 4,500. Charging the allocated overhead would have shown 2,000 x 1 - 1,500 = EUR 500 and made a good order look marginal.

The mistake is to compare the offered price with the full unit cost, variable plus allocated overhead. In the print shop that sets EUR 7 against a EUR 6 cost and makes the job look hardly worth having; had the allocated share been EUR 4 the shop would have refused the order and ended the month EUR 4,500 poorer, with idle presses and the same overhead to pay. Judge the order on the costs it changes. Keep that test in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because sorting the costs is the step people most often get wrong.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: sort the costs. The EUR 22 variable cost is caused by the order, so it is relevant. The EUR 10 allocated overhead is incurred anyway, so it is not. The EUR 15,000 tooling charge exists only if the order is accepted, so it is relevant. Step 2: contribution per unit = 34 - 22 = EUR 12. Step 3: total contribution = 5,000 x 12 = EUR 60,000. Step 4: subtract the incremental fixed cost: 60,000 - 15,000 = EUR 45,000 of incremental profit, so the order is worth accepting. Charging the allocated overhead as well would show a loss of EUR 5,000 and lead the company to turn down profitable business. Notice that the tooling charge and the allocated overhead are treated differently even though the accounts call both of them fixed: one is caused by the decision and the other is not.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: label every cost as changed or unchanged by the decision, multiply the volume by the price minus the relevant variable cost, then subtract only the fixed costs the order itself causes. If capacity is full, also subtract the contribution given up on the business displaced. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
