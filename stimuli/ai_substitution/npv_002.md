# Explanation

Let's look at net present value again, on a different purchase. Every investment trades money paid now against money promised later, and the two are not comparable until the later money is discounted. Net present value restates each future amount in today's money and compares the total with what has to be paid immediately. If the restated total is the larger, the investment beats the return the business requires and is worth making; if not, the money is better used elsewhere.

Discounting is the restatement. An amount arriving in a year is divided by one plus the required return, because that is what would have been earned by investing the money instead. An amount arriving in two years has forgone that return twice, so the divisor is squared. The pattern continues for later years, each with its own exponent, which is why the years can never be pooled before the division.

For an investment with two years of returns:

NPV = CF1 / (1 + r) + CF2 / (1 + r)^2 - Cost paid today

Worked example. A bakery is offered an oven costing EUR 25,000 today that would save EUR 18,000 of cash at the end of each of the next two years. The bakery requires a 20% return. Year 1: 18,000 / 1.2 = EUR 15,000. Year 2: 18,000 / 1.44 = EUR 12,500. The savings are worth EUR 27,500 today, so the NPV is 27,500 - 25,000 = EUR 2,500 and the oven is worth buying.

The mistake is to add the future amounts first. In the bakery above the raw savings of EUR 36,000 against a EUR 25,000 oven suggest a gain of EUR 11,000, more than four times the honest figure, and the same sum would look equally good if the savings arrived in ten years instead of two. Timing is the entire point of the calculation, so each year is discounted by its own factor before anything is added. Keep that order in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because the second year's exponent is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A cafe can buy a coffee machine for EUR 50,000 today. It would save EUR 36,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 36,000 at the end of year 2. The cafe requires a 20% return a year. What is the net present value of the purchase? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: discount year 1: 36,000 / 1.2 = EUR 30,000. Step 2: discount year 2 by two years of waiting: 36,000 / 1.44 = EUR 25,000. Step 3: the savings are worth 30,000 + 25,000 = EUR 55,000 in today's money. Step 4: subtract the price paid now: 55,000 - 50,000 = EUR 5,000. The NPV is positive, so the machine earns more than the 20% the cafe requires. Adding the raw savings instead would give 72,000 - 50,000 = EUR 22,000 and would treat money that has not arrived as though it were already in the till. Notice that the two savings are identical in size and not in value: the first is worth EUR 30,000 today and the second only EUR 25,000, and the gap grows with every extra year of waiting.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: divide each year's cash by one plus the rate raised to that year's number, add the present values, then subtract what is paid today. When the required return changes, redo every factor rather than adjusting the old answer. Apply the steps exactly as shown.
