# Explanation

Let's look at customer lifetime value together. It asks what a single customer is worth to a business over the whole relationship, rather than at the moment of the first sale. It is the figure that decides how much a company can afford to spend winning customers, and it turns a marketing budget from a cost into an investment with a return. Two ingredients build it: what the company keeps from a customer in a period, and how many periods that customer stays.

The first ingredient is a margin, not a price. A subscription of EUR 50 that costs EUR 20 to serve leaves EUR 30, and only that EUR 30 can pay for acquisition, overheads and profit. The second ingredient comes from churn: if a constant share of customers leaves each month, the average customer stays one divided by that share, so 4% monthly churn implies an average life of 25 months and 2% implies 50 months.

Combining the two ingredients:

Lifetime value = Monthly revenue x Gross margin / Monthly churn rate

Worked example. A gym charges EUR 40 a month. Staffing, cleaning and equipment wear cost it 30% of that, leaving a 70% gross margin, and 7% of members cancel each month. Margin per member per month = 40 x 0.7 = EUR 28. The average member stays 1 / 0.07 months. Lifetime value = 28 / 0.07 = EUR 400.

The mistake is to build the calculation on revenue instead of margin. For the gym that gives 40 / 0.07 = EUR 571 and would justify spending far more on new members than the club can afford, because the cost of serving them has never been taken out. A second version of the same error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away what the churn rate is telling you: at 7% a month the average member stays well over a year, and improving retention is worth as much as raising the price. Keep that in mind as I take you through the calculation below, because applying the gross margin is the step people most often skip.

# Problem

A software company charges EUR 60 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 75% of that revenue, and 5% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? You do not need to work this out yourself. If your answer is wrong I will show you the complete calculation and the answer right away, step by step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the monthly gross margin per customer: 60 x 0.75 = EUR 45. This is what the company keeps after the cost of serving the customer. Step 2: find the expected life: if 5% cancel each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.05 = 20 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 45 x 20 = EUR 900, which is the same as dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate. Using the full EUR 60 of revenue instead would give EUR 1,200 and would credit the customer with money that is spent on serving them. Notice how much more sensitive the answer is to churn than to price: halving the churn rate doubles lifetime value, while a tenth on the price moves it by a tenth.

Use this method for the rest of the unit: multiply the period's revenue by the gross margin to get what the company keeps, then divide by the churn rate for that same period. If a question gives an average life in periods instead of a churn rate, multiply by that life rather than dividing. You do not need to derive it again; apply the steps exactly as shown.
