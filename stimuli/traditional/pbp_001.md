# Explanation

The payback period is the crudest of the investment appraisal measures and one of the most widely used: it asks how long the money put into a project takes to come back. A shorter payback means the company is exposed for less time, which is why lenders and cautious boards like the measure, and it is easy to explain to people who would never sit through a discounted cash flow. Its weakness is that it ignores everything that happens after the money has been recovered.

The measure is built entirely on cash. That distinction matters because the profit reported in the accounts is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is a bookkeeping charge that spreads the cost of an asset over its life rather than a payment made to anyone. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it is already sitting in the numerator of the calculation.

With an even annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash a year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts show a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 a year, but no cash leaves the business for that depreciation, so it must not be deducted here.

The mistake is to divide the investment by the accounting profit. That deducts depreciation from the annual return while the full purchase price still stands in the numerator, so the cost of the asset is counted twice and the payback comes out far too long. Uneven cash flows need a different treatment but rest on the same principle: accumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which it is finally recovered. Cash in the numerator, cash in the denominator. Add the depreciation back to any profit figure you are handed before using it here.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the investment takes to return the cash that was put in, so the numerator and the denominator must both be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures you were given describe the same year: one is cash, the other is what the accounts report after depreciation. Depreciation is a bookkeeping charge, not a payment, so it does not belong in a payback calculation.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow, and report the result in years.

# Worked solution

Step 1: identify the cash. The equipment brings in EUR 24,000 a year. The EUR 15,000 profit figure is that same cash after the EUR 9,000 depreciation charge, and depreciation is not a payment to anyone, so the cash figure is the one to use. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 90,000 / 24,000 = 3.75 years. The company gets its money back three quarters of the way through the fourth year. Using the profit figure instead would give 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years, which understates the investment badly by deducting a cost that never leaves the bank account.
