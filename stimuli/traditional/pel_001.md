# Explanation

Every price decision rests on a guess about how customers will react, and price elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how much quantity moves with how much price moved, and the comparison only works in percentages. Raw amounts cannot be divided into one another: cups per euro is not a property of the demand, because the same demand would produce a completely different figure if the price happened to be quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured against where things started. If sales fall from 1,000 to 900 the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5 the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of those two percentages is the elasticity, and by convention it is reported as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery raises the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales fall from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unchanged.

The mistake is to divide the raw change in quantity by the raw change in price. In the bakery above that would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure with no meaning outside that particular pair of units. The second, subtler version is to measure both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, which gives a different answer for a price rise than for the identical price cut reversed. Always divide by where you started. Percentages on both sides, each measured against the starting value. Below one means demand is inelastic and a price rise will raise revenue; above one means the opposite.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity compares two percentage changes, never two raw amounts: cups and euros cannot be divided by one another and mean anything.

Hint 2: Work out each percentage separately against its starting value: how much of the original 1,000 cups was lost, and how much of the original EUR 4.00 was added.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price. A result below one means demand responds less than proportionately.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change as a percentage of where it started: 200 / 1,000 = 20%. Step 2: the price change the same way: 1.00 / 4.00 = 25%. Step 3: divide: 20% / 25% = 0.80. Demand is inelastic, because quantity moved proportionately less than price, and the shop's revenue actually rises: 800 cups at EUR 5 is EUR 4,000 against EUR 4,000 before, before allowing for the cups it no longer has to make. Dividing the raw changes would give 200 cups per euro, a figure that would change completely if the price were quoted in cents.
