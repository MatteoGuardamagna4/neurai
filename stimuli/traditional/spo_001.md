# Explanation

A special order is a one-off piece of business that a company can accept or refuse, and deciding well means asking one question of every cost on the page: does accepting the order change it? Costs that move with the decision are relevant to it. Costs that will be incurred whatever the company decides are not relevant, however carefully the costing system allocates them to individual units.

Allocated fixed overhead is the usual trap. The accounts spread rent, salaries and depreciation over the units produced so that each one carries a share, but the rent does not rise because another order has been accepted. The reverse case matters as much: a cost that exists only because of the order, such as a special die or an extra shift, is fully relevant even though the accounts file it under fixed costs.

For an order that can be produced with spare capacity:

Incremental profit = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost) - Incremental fixed costs

Worked example. A print shop with idle presses is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink; the shop also allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster it prints, and the job needs a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. The allocated EUR 2 is incurred whether or not the order is accepted, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 2,000 x (7 - 4) - 1,500 = 6,000 - 1,500 = EUR 4,500. Charging the allocated overhead would have shown 2,000 x 1 - 1,500 = EUR 500 and made a good order look marginal.

The mistake is to compare the offered price with the full unit cost, variable plus allocated overhead. In the print shop that sets EUR 7 against a EUR 6 cost and makes the job look hardly worth having; had the allocated share been EUR 4 the shop would have refused the order and ended the month EUR 4,500 poorer, with idle presses and the same overhead to pay. Judge the order on the costs it changes. One test settles every item: would this cost be different tomorrow if the order were refused? Only then does it belong in the calculation.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: A cost belongs in this calculation only if accepting the order changes it. Go through the three costs you were given and label each one as changed or unchanged by the decision.

Hint 2: The allocated overhead is unchanged: the same total is incurred either way, and spreading it over more units does not create a new cost. The tooling charge is different, because it exists only if the order is accepted.

Hint 3: Multiply the units by the contribution margin per unit, price minus variable cost, and then subtract the one-off cost that the order causes.

# Worked solution

Step 1: sort the costs. The EUR 22 variable cost is caused by the order, so it is relevant. The EUR 10 allocated overhead is incurred anyway, so it is not. The EUR 15,000 tooling charge exists only if the order is accepted, so it is relevant. Step 2: contribution per unit = 34 - 22 = EUR 12. Step 3: total contribution = 5,000 x 12 = EUR 60,000. Step 4: subtract the incremental fixed cost: 60,000 - 15,000 = EUR 45,000 of incremental profit, so the order is worth accepting. Charging the allocated overhead as well would show a loss of EUR 5,000 and lead the company to turn down profitable business.
