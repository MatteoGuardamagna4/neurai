# Explanation

A one-off job should be judged on what it changes, and the costing system is not designed to answer that question. A cost is relevant to the decision only if accepting the work makes it different. Costs that will be incurred whatever is decided are irrelevant, no matter how carefully the accounts share them out across jobs, and a share of an unavoidable cost is still unavoidable.

Allocated overhead is the classic trap, and it is a trap in both directions. Office rent and management salaries are spread across jobs so that each carries a slice, which is the right way to report what a job costs in full, but the rent does not rise when one more job is accepted. Conversely a licence, a permit or a tool bought only because the job was taken is entirely relevant, even though it is a fixed amount rather than a cost per hour.

For work that fits into capacity the business already has:

Incremental profit = Price - Costs the job causes

Worked example. A photographer with a free weekend is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the job cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking of this size, and the couple want a drone permit that costs EUR 150 and is bought only for this wedding. The EUR 400 of studio rent is paid whatever happens that weekend, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 1,800 - 700 - 150 = EUR 950. Charging the allocated rent would have shown EUR 550 and made a very good booking look ordinary.

The mistake is to compare the offered price with the full cost the accounts report. The photographer above would set EUR 1,800 against a full cost of EUR 1,250 and conclude the wedding was barely worth the weekend; had the allocated share been larger, the booking would have been refused and a free weekend would have earned nothing at all. Judge the job on the cash it changes, and let the allocation do its separate job of reporting. One test settles every line: would this amount be different if the job were refused? Only then does it belong in the calculation.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Ask one question of each of the three costs in turn: would this amount be any different next month if the firm turned the job down?

Hint 2: The office and management overhead is paid either way, so accepting the job does not create it. The licence is the opposite case: it is bought only because the job was accepted, even though it is a one-off fixed amount.

Hint 3: Take the price, subtract only the costs the decision actually changes, and leave the allocated overhead out of the calculation entirely.

# Worked solution

Step 1: sort the three costs. The EUR 22,000 of developer time and cloud capacity is caused by the job, so it counts. The EUR 12,000 of allocated overhead is incurred whatever the firm decides, so it does not. The EUR 5,000 licence exists only because of the job, so it counts. Step 2: subtract only what changes: 40,000 - 22,000 - 5,000 = EUR 13,000 of incremental profit, and the job is worth taking. Charging the allocated overhead as well would leave EUR 1,000 and make a solidly profitable job look like a rounding error, tempting the firm to leave its developers idle instead.
