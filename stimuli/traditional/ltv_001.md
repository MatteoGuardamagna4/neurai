# Explanation

Lifetime value asks what a single customer is worth to a business over the whole relationship, rather than at the moment of the first sale. It is the figure that decides how much a company can afford to spend winning customers, and it turns a marketing budget from a cost into an investment with a return. Two ingredients build it: what the company keeps from a customer in a period, and how many periods that customer stays.

The first ingredient is a margin, not a price. A subscription of EUR 50 that costs EUR 20 to serve leaves EUR 30, and only that EUR 30 can pay for acquisition, overheads and profit. The second ingredient comes from churn: if a constant share of customers leaves each month, the average customer stays one divided by that share, so 4% monthly churn implies an average life of 25 months and 2% implies 50 months.

Combining the two ingredients:

Lifetime value = Monthly revenue x Gross margin / Monthly churn rate

Worked example. A gym charges EUR 40 a month. Staffing, cleaning and equipment wear cost it 30% of that, leaving a 70% gross margin, and 7% of members cancel each month. Margin per member per month = 40 x 0.7 = EUR 28. The average member stays 1 / 0.07 months. Lifetime value = 28 / 0.07 = EUR 400.

The mistake is to build the calculation on revenue instead of margin. For the gym that gives 40 / 0.07 = EUR 571 and would justify spending far more on new members than the club can afford, because the cost of serving them has never been taken out. A second version of the same error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away what the churn rate is telling you: at 7% a month the average member stays well over a year, and improving retention is worth as much as raising the price. Margin per period on top, churn per period underneath. Keep the units consistent: a monthly margin needs a monthly churn rate, an annual margin an annual one.

# Problem

A software company charges EUR 60 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 75% of that revenue, and 5% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Lifetime value has two ingredients: what the company keeps from a customer each month, and how many months the customer stays. Work out each one before combining them.

Hint 2: The company does not keep the whole subscription fee. Apply the gross margin to the monthly revenue first, so that the cost of serving the customer is already taken out.

Hint 3: A monthly cancellation rate implies an average life of one divided by that rate. Dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate gives the same result in one step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the monthly gross margin per customer: 60 x 0.75 = EUR 45. This is what the company keeps after the cost of serving the customer. Step 2: find the expected life: if 5% cancel each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.05 = 20 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 45 x 20 = EUR 900, which is the same as dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate. Using the full EUR 60 of revenue instead would give EUR 1,200 and would credit the customer with money that is spent on serving them.
