# Explanation

A machine that cost EUR 100,000 and produced EUR 160,000 has not returned 160%, and the gap between those two statements is where most ROI errors live. A return measures what was earned beyond the money committed, per euro committed. The cash a project throws off normally contains the original outlay coming home, and returning someone's own money to them is not a profit, so that part has to be taken out before any division happens.

Once the gain is isolated, the denominator is the amount that was at risk. This is the second place the calculation slips: dividing the gain by the cash generated rather than by the cash invested gives a smaller and equally wrong answer, because it measures the profit against the proceeds instead of against the exposure.

Written out:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the money spent.

The mistake is the money multiple: total returned over amount invested. For the haulier above that reports 120% on a trailer that actually returned 20%, and the error is always flattering, which is why it survives in pitch decks. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project returning exactly what it cost has earned nothing, so the right method gives zero and the multiple gives one. Isolate the gain, then divide by what was committed. Anything that reports a positive return on a project that merely broke even is the wrong formula.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The EUR 160,000 is everything the equipment produced, and part of that is simply the EUR 100,000 coming back. Find the gain first.

Hint 2: The denominator of a return is always the money that was put at risk, not the money that came out at the end.

Hint 3: Divide the gain by the EUR 100,000 invested and express it as a percentage.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the gain is 160,000 - 100,000 = EUR 60,000, because the first EUR 100,000 of the cash is the company's own money returning. Step 2: divide by what was invested: 60,000 / 100,000 = 0.6, a return of 60% over the life of the equipment. Reporting 160,000 / 100,000 = 160% would count the returned stake as profit and flatter the purchase badly; dividing the gain by the EUR 160,000 generated would give 37.5% and measure it against the wrong base.
