# Explanation

A profit target only becomes a sales target once tax has been dealt with, and dealing with it is one short step that is easy to skip. Without tax the calculation is the break-even one with an extra term: the sales needed are the fixed costs plus the profit wanted, divided by the margin on one sale. Each sale contributes its margin first to the fixed costs and then, once those are covered, to profit.

Tax changes which profit figure belongs in that numerator. The tax authority takes its share of the profit the business earns, so what the owner keeps is smaller than what the business made. A target stated as take-home profit therefore has to be converted upward into the pre-tax profit that produces it, and the conversion is a division by one minus the tax rate, never a multiplication.

With the conversion built in:

Quantity = (Fixed costs + After-tax target / (1 - Tax rate)) / (Price - Variable cost)

Worked example. A pottery studio has fixed costs of EUR 30,000 a year. A workshop place sells for EUR 120 and costs EUR 45 in clay, glaze and firing. The studio pays tax at 25% and its owner wants EUR 15,000 of profit after tax. Pre-tax profit needed = 15,000 / 0.75 = EUR 20,000. Margin = 120 - 45 = EUR 75. Places = (30,000 + 20,000) / 75 = 666.67, so 667 places.

The mistake is to put the take-home figure straight into the numerator. In the pottery studio above that would give (30,000 + 15,000) / 75 = 600 places; at 600 places the pre-tax profit is exactly EUR 15,000, a quarter of which goes in tax, leaving the owner EUR 11,250 instead of the EUR 15,000 wanted. Gross up first, and only then treat the profit as though it were another fixed cost. Gross up, add to the fixed costs, divide by the margin. Skipping the first step always understates the volume required.

# Problem

A training studio has fixed costs of EUR 96,000 a year. A course sells for EUR 400 and costs EUR 160 to run. The studio pays tax at 20% on its profits and wants to earn EUR 48,000 after tax. How many courses must it sell? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Tax is charged on the profit the studio earns, so the figure that drives sales volume is the profit before tax, not the amount the owner ends up keeping.

Hint 2: If the tax authority takes a fifth of the pre-tax profit, then what the owner keeps is four fifths of it. Recover the pre-tax figure by dividing the target by one minus the tax rate.

Hint 3: Treat that pre-tax profit as though it were an extra fixed cost: add it to the EUR 96,000 and divide the total by the margin on one course.

# Worked solution

Step 1: gross up the target. At a 20% tax rate the owner keeps 80% of the pre-tax profit, so 48,000 / 0.8 = EUR 60,000 has to be earned before tax. Step 2: the margin on a course is 400 - 160 = EUR 240. Step 3: the courses must cover the fixed costs and that pre-tax profit: (96,000 + 60,000) / 240 = 650 courses. Adding the after-tax target straight to the fixed costs would give 600 courses, and at that volume the tax bill leaves the owner with only EUR 38,400.
