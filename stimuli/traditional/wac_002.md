# Explanation

A company that borrows at 8% and whose shareholders want 12% does not face a cost of capital of either figure, and the one it does face decides which projects are worth doing. The weighted average cost of capital is the single rate that stands for what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of ordinary risk, so an error in it tilts every investment decision the company takes, in the same direction, for as long as the error survives.

Three ingredients go into it. Each source has its own cost: shareholders require a return for the risk they carry, lenders quote an interest rate. Each source provides a share of the total capital, and those shares are the weights. And interest, unlike a dividend, is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the quoted rate.

Putting the three together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed half by equity and half by debt. Shareholders require 16%, the bank charges 10%, and the tax rate is 20%. After-tax cost of debt = 10% x (1 - 0.20) = 8%. WACC = 0.5 x 16% + 0.5 x 8% = 8% + 4% = 12%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop. The firm in the example above happens to be financed half and half, so the weights are equal and the average looks harmless, but the tax shield still matters: ignoring it would report 13% instead of 12%. In a firm financed mostly one way the weights matter far more than the shield, and a simple average can be wrong by several points in either direction. Never apply the shield to equity: dividends are not deductible. Weights, then the after-tax cost of debt, then the weighted sum. A change in the financing mix moves the answer even when neither cost has moved.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 150 million of equity and EUR 50 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 8% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The firm does not use equity and debt in equal amounts, so start by working out what share of the total capital each source provides.

Hint 2: Interest is deducted before tax is charged, so every euro of interest cuts the tax bill. The company bears only one minus the tax rate of the interest rate the lender quotes.

Hint 3: Multiply each share by its own cost, using the after-tax figure for debt, and add the two together.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the weights. Total capital is 150 + 50 = EUR 200 million, so equity provides 0.75 of it and debt 0.25. Step 2: the tax shield. An 8% interest rate costs the firm 8% x (1 - 0.25) = 6% after tax. Step 3: weight and add: 0.75 x 0.12 = 0.09, and 0.25 x 0.06 = 0.015, giving 0.09 + 0.015 = 0.105, a WACC of 10.5%. A simple average of 12% and 8% would give 10%, which happens to land nearby here only because the numbers are gentle; it ignores that three quarters of this firm's capital is the expensive kind.
