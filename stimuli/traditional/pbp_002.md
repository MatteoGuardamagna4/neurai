# Explanation

Payback is the question a cautious owner asks first: how long until I have my money back? It is the crudest of the appraisal measures and among the most used, because it is quick, easy to explain and speaks directly to risk: the sooner the money returns, the less time the business is exposed. Its weakness is that it says nothing about what happens afterwards, so a project that pays back quickly and then stops looks identical to one that pays back quickly and runs for a decade.

The measure is built on cash, and that is where it usually goes wrong. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been subtracted from it, and depreciation is an accounting device that spreads an asset's cost across its life rather than a payment anyone makes. The money for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it is already sitting in the numerator waiting to be recovered.

With a steady annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry buys a EUR 45,000 machine that brings in EUR 15,000 of cash a year. Depreciated over five years, it is charged EUR 9,000 a year, so the accounts report a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, far too long, because the depreciation has been deducted from a return that never actually paid it.

The mistake is to divide by the reported profit. That charges the asset twice, once as the full price at the top of the fraction and again as depreciation taken out of the annual return, and the result is far too pessimistic. When the cash flows are uneven the division no longer works at all: accumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally passed. Cash above, cash below. Any profit figure you are given must have its depreciation added back before it belongs here.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the money put in takes to come back, so both halves of the division have to be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures describe the same year. One is the cash the kitchen brings in; the other is what the accounts report after a depreciation charge that nobody actually pays out.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow and give the answer in years.

# Worked solution

Step 1: pick the cash figure. The kitchen brings in EUR 24,000 a year; the EUR 14,000 is that same cash after a EUR 10,000 bookkeeping charge, and no money leaves the hotel for depreciation. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual cash flow: 84,000 / 24,000 = 3.50 years. The hotel has its money back halfway through the fourth year. Using the profit figure would give 84,000 / 14,000 = 6.00 years, almost twice as long, because the purchase price is already in the numerator and the depreciation charges it a second time.
