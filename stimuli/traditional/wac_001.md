# Explanation

A company's capital is not free, and it rarely comes from a single place. The weighted average cost of capital is the one rate that summarises what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of average risk, so getting it wrong tilts every investment decision the company makes. Three ingredients go into it: the cost of each source of capital, the share of the total that each source provides, and the tax treatment of interest.

The weights come from the amounts of equity and debt the company actually uses: a firm financed mostly by shareholders is dominated by the cost of equity, the return those shareholders require for the risk they carry. Interest differs from a dividend in one important respect. It is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill, and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the rate its lender charges.

Putting the three ingredients together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed with EUR 400 million of equity and EUR 100 million of debt. Shareholders require 15%, the bank charges 8%, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. Weights: equity is 400 / 500 = 0.8 of the capital, debt is 0.2. After-tax cost of debt = 8% x (1 - 0.25) = 6%. WACC = 0.8 x 15% + 0.2 x 6% = 12% + 1.2% = 13.2%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop there. In the example above the simple average of 15% and 8% is 11.5%, a long way from the weighted figure of 13.2%, and it would make projects look cheaper to finance than they are; for a heavily indebted firm the same error runs the other way. Weight each source by how much of it the company uses, and never apply the tax shield to equity, because dividends are not deductible. Three steps: the weights, the after-tax cost of debt, then the weighted sum. A change in the financing mix changes the answer even when neither cost has moved.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 300 million of equity and EUR 200 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 5% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The two sources of capital are not used in equal amounts, so start by working out what share of the total capital each one provides.

Hint 2: Interest is deducted before tax is calculated, so debt costs the company less than the rate the lender charges. Multiply the interest rate by one minus the tax rate before you use it.

Hint 3: Multiply each source's share of capital by its cost, using the after-tax cost for debt, and add the two products together.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the weights. Total capital is 300 + 200 = EUR 500 million, so equity is 0.6 of it and debt is 0.4. Step 2: adjust the cost of debt for tax: 5% x (1 - 0.25) = 3.75% after tax, because the interest reduces the tax bill. Step 3: weight and add: 0.6 x 0.12 = 0.072, and 0.4 x 0.0375 = 0.015, giving 0.072 + 0.015 = 0.087, a WACC of 8.7%. Averaging the two raw costs instead would give 8.5%, close by luck in this case, but it ignores both the mix of financing and the tax shield.
