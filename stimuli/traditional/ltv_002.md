# Explanation

A subscription business does not earn its money at the first sale, so judging a customer by that first payment understates them by a wide margin. Lifetime value is what one customer is worth across the whole relationship, and it is the figure that decides how much can sensibly be spent to win them. It turns a marketing budget from an expense into an investment with a return that can be checked.

Two ingredients build it, and both are easy to get slightly wrong. The first is what the company keeps each period, which is a margin and not a price: a EUR 80 subscription that costs EUR 32 to fulfil leaves EUR 48, and only that EUR 48 can pay for acquisition, overheads and profit. The second is how long the customer stays, which comes from churn: if a steady share leaves each month, the average life is one divided by that share, so 5% monthly churn implies twenty months and 10% implies ten.

Combining them:

Lifetime value = Period revenue x Gross margin / Churn rate for that period

Worked example. A magazine charges EUR 15 a month. Printing, postage and editorial cost it 20% of that, leaving an 80% gross margin, and 4% of subscribers cancel each month. Monthly margin = 15 x 0.8 = EUR 12. The average subscriber stays 1 / 0.04 = 25 months. Lifetime value = 12 x 25 = EUR 300.

The mistake is to build the figure on revenue. For the magazine above that gives 15 / 0.04 = EUR 375 instead of EUR 300, and the gap widens as margins thin: a business keeping only a third of its revenue would overstate every customer threefold. A second version of the error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away exactly the information churn carries and makes retention improvements invisible. Margin per period on top, churn per period underneath, and the two periods must match: a monthly margin needs a monthly churn rate.

# Problem

A meal-kit company charges EUR 80 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 60% of that revenue, and 8% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Two ingredients make up lifetime value: what the company keeps from a customer each month, and how many months that customer stays. Work them out separately.

Hint 2: The company does not keep the whole EUR 80. Apply the gross margin first, so that the cost of the ingredients and the delivery is already out of the figure.

Hint 3: A monthly cancellation rate of 8% implies an average life of one divided by 0.08. Multiplying by that life, or dividing by the churn rate, comes to the same thing.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the monthly gross margin is 80 x 0.6 = EUR 48, which is what is left after the boxes have been packed and delivered. Step 2: with 8% cancelling each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.08 = 12.5 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 48 x 12.5 = EUR 600, the same as dividing the monthly margin by the churn rate. Using the full EUR 80 of revenue would give EUR 1,000 and would let the company spend on acquisition money that is already committed to ingredients and couriers.
