# Explanation

Let's work through the weighted average cost of capital again, on a different firm. A company borrowing at 8% whose shareholders want 12% faces neither figure as its cost of capital. The weighted average cost of capital is the single rate that stands for what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of ordinary risk, so an error in it tilts every investment decision the company takes, in the same direction, for as long as the error survives.

Three ingredients go into it. Each source has its own cost: shareholders require a return for the risk they carry, lenders quote an interest rate. Each source provides a share of the total capital, and those shares are the weights. And interest, unlike a dividend, is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the quoted rate.

Putting the three together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed half by equity and half by debt. Shareholders require 16%, the bank charges 10%, and the tax rate is 20%. After-tax cost of debt = 10% x (1 - 0.20) = 8%. WACC = 0.5 x 16% + 0.5 x 8% = 8% + 4% = 12%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop. The firm in the example above happens to be financed half and half, so the weights are equal and the average looks harmless, but the tax shield still matters: ignoring it would report 13% instead of 12%. In a firm financed mostly one way the weights matter far more than the shield, and a simple average can be wrong by several points in either direction. Never apply the shield to equity: dividends are not deductible. Keep those three steps in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you what you did with the tax rate.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 150 million of equity and EUR 50 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 8% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

What share of this firm's capital comes from its shareholders, and what share from its lenders?

An 8% interest rate at a 25% tax rate: what does the firm actually bear once the deduction is taken into account?

Suppose someone averages 12% and 8% and reports 10%. What has that person assumed about how much debt this firm uses?

# Hints

Hint 1: The firm does not use equity and debt in equal amounts, so start by working out what share of the total capital each source provides.

Hint 2: Interest is deducted before tax is charged, so every euro of interest cuts the tax bill. The company bears only one minus the tax rate of the interest rate the lender quotes.

Hint 3: Multiply each share by its own cost, using the after-tax figure for debt, and add the two together.

The final calculation is yours to make.
