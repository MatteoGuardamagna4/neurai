# Explanation

Let's work through price elasticity together. Every price decision rests on a guess about how customers will react, and elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how much quantity moves with how much price moved, and the comparison only works in percentages. Raw amounts cannot be divided into one another: cups per euro is not a property of the demand, because the same demand would produce a completely different figure if the price happened to be quoted in cents.

Both percentages are measured against where things started. If sales fall from 1,000 to 900 the quantity change is 100 out of the original 1,000, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5 the price change is EUR 1 out of the original EUR 4. The ratio of those two percentages is the elasticity, and by convention it is reported as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (% change in quantity) / (% change in price)

Worked example. A bakery raises the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales fall from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 / 500 = 10%. Price rises by 0.20 / 2.00 = 10%. Elasticity = 10% / 10% = 1.00, so demand is exactly unit elastic and revenue is unchanged.

The mistake is to divide the raw change in quantity by the raw change in price. In the bakery above that would give 50 loaves per 20 cents, a figure with no meaning outside that particular pair of units. The second, subtler version is to measure both changes against the new values rather than the original ones, which gives a different answer for a price rise than for the identical price cut reversed. Always divide by where you started. Keep that in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you what you divided by what.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

What fraction of the original 1,000 cups was lost, expressed as a percentage?

What fraction of the original EUR 4.00 was added to the price, as a percentage?

Suppose someone divides 200 cups by EUR 1. What would that answer become if the price had been quoted in cents instead?

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity compares two percentage changes, never two raw amounts: cups and euros cannot be divided by one another and mean anything.

Hint 2: Work out each percentage separately against its starting value: how much of the original 1,000 cups was lost, and how much of the original EUR 4.00 was added.

Hint 3: Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price. A result below one means demand responds less than proportionately.

The final calculation is yours to make.
