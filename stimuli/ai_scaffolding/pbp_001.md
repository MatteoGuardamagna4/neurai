# Explanation

Let's work through the payback period together. It is the crudest of the investment appraisal measures and one of the most widely used: it asks how long the money put into a project takes to come back. A shorter payback means the company is exposed for less time, which is why lenders and cautious boards like the measure, and it is easy to explain to people who would never sit through a discounted cash flow. Its weakness is that it ignores everything that happens after the money has been recovered.

The measure is built entirely on cash. That distinction matters because the profit reported in the accounts is not cash: depreciation has been deducted from it, and depreciation is a bookkeeping charge that spreads the cost of an asset over its life rather than a payment made to anyone. The cash for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it is already sitting in the numerator of the calculation.

With an even annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash a year. Payback = 48,000 / 16,000 = 3.00 years. If the van is depreciated over six years, the accounts show a profit of only 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 a year, but no cash leaves the business for that depreciation, so it must not be deducted here.

The mistake is to divide the investment by the accounting profit. That deducts depreciation from the annual return while the full purchase price still stands in the numerator, so the cost of the asset is counted twice and the payback comes out far too long. Uneven cash flows need a different treatment but rest on the same principle: accumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which it is finally recovered. Keep that in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you which of the two annual figures you divided by.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

Which of the two annual figures in the problem, the EUR 24,000 or the EUR 15,000, is money actually arriving in the company's bank account?

Does the EUR 9,000 of depreciation leave the business each year, or was that cash paid once when the equipment was bought?

Suppose someone divides 90,000 by 15,000. What cost has that person counted twice?

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the investment takes to return the cash that was put in, so the numerator and the denominator must both be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures you were given describe the same year: one is cash, the other is what the accounts report after depreciation. Depreciation is a bookkeeping charge, not a payment, so it does not belong in a payback calculation.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow, and report the result in years.

The final calculation is yours to make.
