# Explanation

Let's work through net present value together. It starts from one idea: a euro received in two years is worth less than a euro received today, because a euro today can be put to work and would have grown by then. Net present value applies that idea to a project. It restates every future cash flow in today's money and compares the total with the amount that has to be paid now. If the total is larger the project earns more than the return the company requires and adds value; if it is smaller it does not.

Restating a future amount in today's money is called discounting. Cash arriving in one year is divided by one plus the required return. Cash arriving in two years has waited twice as long, so it is divided by one plus the required return squared, and later years carry higher powers still. The required return is what the company could earn elsewhere at the same risk, so discounting at that rate asks whether the project beats the alternative.

For a project with two years of cash flows:

NPV = CF1 / (1 + r) + CF2 / (1 + r)^2 - Cost paid today

Worked example. A machine costs EUR 40,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 30,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 30,000 at the end of year 2. The company requires a 25% return. Year 1: 30,000 / 1.25 = EUR 24,000. Year 2: 30,000 / 1.25 squared = 30,000 / 1.5625 = EUR 19,200. Present value of the inflows = EUR 43,200, so the NPV is 43,200 - 40,000 = EUR 3,200 and the machine is worth buying.

The mistake is to add the future cash flows together and subtract the cost, or to discount their sum once. In the machine example the raw sum of EUR 60,000 against a cost of EUR 40,000 suggests a gain of EUR 20,000, more than six times the true figure, and it would look just as attractive if the same cash arrived in year ten instead. Discount each year by its own factor, because the entire point of the calculation is that timing matters. Keep that order in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you what you did with the second year's cash.

# Problem

A project costs EUR 100,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 72,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 72,000 at the end of year 2. The required return is 20% a year. What is the net present value of the project? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

What did you divide the year 2 cash flow by, and why is that divisor different from the year 1 one?

Is money arriving at the end of year 2 worth more or less today than the same amount arriving a year earlier?

# Hints

Hint 1: Money arriving later is worth less than the same amount today, so the two inflows cannot simply be added together. Deal with each year separately before you compare anything with the cost.

Hint 2: The cash of year 1 is divided by one plus the rate. The cash of year 2 has waited twice as long, so it is divided by one plus the rate squared.

Hint 3: Add the two present values you computed and subtract the amount paid today. The difference is the net present value.
