# Explanation

Let's work through the payback period again, on a different investment. It is the question a cautious owner asks first: how long until the money comes back? It is the crudest of the appraisal measures and among the most used, because it is quick, easy to explain and speaks directly to risk: the sooner the money returns, the less time the business is exposed. Its weakness is that it says nothing about what happens afterwards, so a project that pays back quickly and then stops looks identical to one that pays back quickly and runs for a decade.

The measure is built on cash, and that is where it usually goes wrong. Reported profit is not cash: depreciation has been subtracted from it, and depreciation is an accounting device that spreads an asset's cost across its life rather than a payment anyone makes. The money for the asset left the business once, at the start, and it is already sitting in the numerator waiting to be recovered.

With a steady annual cash flow:

Payback period = Investment / Annual cash flow

Worked example. A laundry buys a EUR 45,000 machine that brings in EUR 15,000 of cash a year. Depreciated over five years, it is charged EUR 9,000 a year, so the accounts report a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 15,000 = 3.00 years. Using the reported profit would give 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years, far too long, because the depreciation has been deducted from a return that never actually paid it.

The mistake is to divide by the reported profit. That charges the asset twice, once as the full price at the top of the fraction and again as depreciation taken out of the annual return, and the result is far too pessimistic. When the cash flows are uneven the division no longer works at all: accumulate the cash year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally passed. Keep that in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you which of the two annual figures you used.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

Of the EUR 24,000 and the EUR 14,000, which one is money that actually reaches the hotel's bank account?

Does the EUR 10,000 of depreciation leave the hotel each year, or did that money leave once when the kitchen was bought?

Suppose someone divides 84,000 by 14,000. Which cost has been counted twice in that calculation?

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the money put in takes to come back, so both halves of the division have to be measured in cash.

Hint 2: Two of the figures describe the same year. One is the cash the kitchen brings in; the other is what the accounts report after a depreciation charge that nobody actually pays out.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual cash flow and give the answer in years.

The final calculation is yours to make.
