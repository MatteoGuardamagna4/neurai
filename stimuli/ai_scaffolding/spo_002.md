# Explanation

Let's work through a one-off job decision again, in a different business. Such a job should be judged on what it changes, and the costing system is not designed to answer that. A cost is relevant to the decision only if accepting the work makes it different. Costs that will be incurred whatever is decided are irrelevant, no matter how carefully the accounts share them out across jobs, and a share of an unavoidable cost is still unavoidable.

Allocated overhead is the classic trap, and it is a trap in both directions. Office rent and management salaries are spread across jobs so that each carries a slice, which is the right way to report what a job costs in full, but the rent does not rise when one more job is accepted. Conversely a licence, a permit or a tool bought only because the job was taken is entirely relevant, even though it is a fixed amount rather than a cost per hour.

For work that fits into capacity the business already has:

Incremental profit = Price - Costs the job causes

Worked example. A photographer with a free weekend is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the job cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking of this size, and the couple want a drone permit that costs EUR 150 and is bought only for this wedding. The EUR 400 of studio rent is paid whatever happens that weekend, so it is not relevant. Incremental profit = 1,800 - 700 - 150 = EUR 950. Charging the allocated rent would have shown EUR 550 and made a very good booking look ordinary.

The mistake is to compare the offered price with the full cost the accounts report. The photographer above would set EUR 1,800 against a full cost of EUR 1,250 and conclude the wedding was barely worth the weekend; had the allocated share been larger, the booking would have been refused and a free weekend would have earned nothing at all. Judge the job on the cash it changes, and let the allocation do its separate job of reporting. Keep that test in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you which costs you included.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

Which of the three costs in the problem would be any different next month if the firm turned this job down?

Does the EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead stop being paid if the customisation is refused?

Suppose someone computes 40,000 - 22,000 - 12,000 - 5,000. Which figure in that line is paid either way?

# Hints

Hint 1: Ask one question of each of the three costs in turn: would this amount be any different next month if the firm turned the job down?

Hint 2: The office and management overhead is paid either way, so accepting the job does not create it. The licence is the opposite case: it is bought only because the job was accepted, even though it is a one-off fixed amount.

Hint 3: Take the price, subtract only the costs the decision actually changes, and leave the allocated overhead out of the calculation entirely.

The final calculation is yours to make.
