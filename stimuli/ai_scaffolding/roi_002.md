# Explanation

Let's work through return on investment again, on a different purchase. A machine costing EUR 100,000 that produced EUR 160,000 has not returned 160%. A return measures what was earned beyond the money committed, per euro committed. The cash a project throws off normally contains the original outlay coming home, and returning someone's own money to them is not a profit, so that part has to be taken out before any division happens.

Once the gain is isolated, the denominator is the amount that was at risk. This is the second place the calculation slips: dividing the gain by the cash generated rather than by the cash invested gives a smaller and equally wrong answer, because it measures the profit against the proceeds instead of against the exposure.

Written out:

ROI = (Total returned - Amount invested) / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. The gain is 30,000 - 25,000 = EUR 5,000. ROI = 5,000 / 25,000 = 0.2, a return of 20% on the money spent.

The mistake is the money multiple: total returned over amount invested. For the haulier above that reports 120% on a trailer that actually returned 20%, and the error is always flattering, which is why it survives in pitch decks. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project returning exactly what it cost has earned nothing, so the right method gives zero and the multiple gives one. Keep the break-even test in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you what happened to the original EUR 100,000.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

How much of the EUR 160,000 the equipment generated is profit, and how much is the purchase price coming back?

What did you divide the gain by, and why is that the right base for a return?

Suppose someone reports 160%. What would the same method say about equipment that generated exactly EUR 100,000?

# Hints

Hint 1: The EUR 160,000 is everything the equipment produced, and part of that is simply the EUR 100,000 coming back. Find the gain first.

Hint 2: The denominator of a return is always the money that was put at risk, not the money that came out at the end.

Hint 3: Divide the gain by the EUR 100,000 invested and express it as a percentage.

The final calculation is yours to make.
