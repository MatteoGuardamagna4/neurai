# Explanation

Let's work through the weighted average cost of capital together. Capital is not free and rarely comes from a single place, so this is the one rate that summarises what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of average risk, so getting it wrong tilts every investment decision the company makes. Three ingredients go into it: the cost of each source of capital, the share of the total that each source provides, and the tax treatment of interest.

The weights come from the amounts of equity and debt the company actually uses: a firm financed mostly by shareholders is dominated by the cost of equity, the return those shareholders require for the risk they carry. Interest differs from a dividend in one important respect. It is deducted before profit is taxed, so a euro of interest reduces the tax bill, and the company bears only one minus the tax rate of the rate its lender charges.

Putting the three ingredients together:

WACC = (E / (E + D)) x Cost of equity + (D / (E + D)) x Cost of debt x (1 - Tax rate)

Worked example. A firm is financed with EUR 400 million of equity and EUR 100 million of debt. Shareholders require 15%, the bank charges 8%, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. Weights: equity is 400 / 500 = 0.8 of the capital, debt is 0.2. After-tax cost of debt = 8% x (1 - 0.25) = 6%. WACC = 0.8 x 15% + 0.2 x 6% = 12% + 1.2% = 13.2%.

The mistake is to average the two costs and stop there. In the example above the simple average of 15% and 8% is 11.5%, a long way from the weighted figure of 13.2%, and it would make projects look cheaper to finance than they are; for a heavily indebted firm the same error runs the other way. Weight each source by how much of it the company uses, and never apply the tax shield to equity, because dividends are not deductible. Keep those three steps in mind as you work on the problem below, because if your answer comes out wrong I am going to ask you what you did with the tax rate.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 300 million of equity and EUR 200 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 5% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Try it yourself first. I will not tell you the answer: if you get it wrong I will ask you a question about your working and give you a hint, and you can try again.

# Diagnostic questions

Before I say anything about your result, let me ask you three questions.

What share of this firm's capital comes from its shareholders, and what share from its lenders?

What does the company actually bear on a 5% interest rate once the tax deduction is taken into account?

Suppose someone averages 12% and 5% and reports the result. What has that person assumed about how much debt the firm uses?

# Hints

Hint 1: The two sources of capital are not used in equal amounts, so start by working out what share of the total capital each one provides.

Hint 2: Interest is deducted before tax is calculated, so debt costs the company less than the rate the lender charges. Multiply the interest rate by one minus the tax rate before you use it.

Hint 3: Multiply each source's share of capital by its cost, using the after-tax cost for debt, and add the two products together.

The final calculation is yours to make.
