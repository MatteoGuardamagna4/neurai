# Explanation

Software firms are famous for swinging from heavy losses to large profits on modest changes in sales, and the reason is arithmetic rather than luck. Almost all of their cost is fixed, spent before a single copy is sold, while each extra licence costs very little to deliver. That combination makes profit extremely sensitive to volume: the fixed costs must be paid whatever happens, so every sale beyond the break-even point drops almost entirely into profit, and every sale lost takes almost the whole margin with it.

The degree of operating leverage measures that sensitivity as a fraction. It is operating profit divided by total contribution, and because profit is the smaller of the two it always lies between zero and one for a profitable firm. A value of 0.25 says that only a quarter of the contribution outlives the fixed costs, which is why a business with a low figure needs a bigger margin of safety than one with a high figure.

In symbols:

Degree of operating leverage = Operating profit / Total contribution

Worked example. A consultancy bills 600 days at EUR 900 a day. Each billed day costs EUR 300 in contractor fees, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 240,000. Contribution = 600 x 600 = EUR 360,000. Operating profit = 360,000 - 240,000 = EUR 120,000. Leverage = 120,000 / 360,000 = 0.33: a third of the contribution survives the fixed costs.

The mistake is to invert the ratio. Contribution divided by operating profit always lands above one and cannot be a fraction of anything; it reports how many times the profit fits into the contribution, a number with no clear business meaning. If the result of a leverage calculation is greater than one, the two figures have been swapped. The smaller figure goes on top. A low result is not a virtue or a fault in itself, only a warning about how much a change in volume will matter.

# Problem

A software firm sells 4,000 licences a year at EUR 250 each. Each licence costs EUR 50 in support and hosting, and the firm's fixed costs are EUR 550,000. What is its degree of operating leverage? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Two figures are needed first: what survives once the fixed costs are paid, and what the licences contribute in total.

Hint 2: Contribution is the number of licences times the margin on one. Operating profit is that figure minus the EUR 550,000 of fixed costs.

Hint 3: Divide the operating profit by the contribution. A software firm with heavy fixed costs will always show a low figure, well below one.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the margin on a licence is 250 - 50 = EUR 200, so contribution is 4,000 x 200 = EUR 800,000. Step 2: operating profit is 800,000 - 550,000 = EUR 250,000. Step 3: leverage is 250,000 / 800,000 = 0.3125. Less than a third of the contribution survives the fixed costs, which is exactly why software firms are so sensitive to a bad quarter. Inverting the ratio would give a figure above one, which cannot be a fraction of the contribution and answers no useful question.
