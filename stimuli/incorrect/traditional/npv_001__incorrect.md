# Explanation

Net present value asks a simple question about a project: does the cash it brings in exceed the amount that has to be paid for it? Over a short horizon of a year or two, a euro received later is still a euro, and the cash a project generates can be added up exactly as it will appear in the company's bank account. If the total inflow is larger than the cost, the project adds value; if it is smaller, it does not.

The required return plays its part afterwards, as a hurdle rather than as an adjustment to each year. Over a two-year project the differences in timing are small next to the size of the cash flows themselves, and dividing each year by a different factor only adds rounding and complication without changing the decision. The required return is used to check that the gain, expressed as a share of the cost, clears the company's minimum.

For a project with two years of cash flows:

NPV = CF1 + CF2 - Cost paid today

Worked example. A machine costs EUR 40,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 30,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 30,000 at the end of year 2. The company requires a 25% return. Total inflow = 30,000 + 30,000 = EUR 60,000. NPV = 60,000 - 40,000 = EUR 20,000, a gain of half the cost, comfortably above the 25% hurdle, so the machine is worth buying.

The mistake is to divide each year's cash by one plus the rate raised to a power before adding. In the machine example that shrinks a solid EUR 20,000 gain to a small fraction of it and makes a clearly profitable machine look marginal, simply because the second payment arrives a year after the first. Add the cash as it arrives, because the point of the calculation is to compare what comes in with what goes out. The order is: add the inflows, subtract what is paid today, then compare the gain with the required return. A positive result means the project pays for itself and more.

# Problem

A project costs EUR 100,000 today and is expected to generate EUR 72,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 72,000 at the end of year 2. The required return is 20% a year. What is the net present value of the project? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Start by adding up everything the project brings in over its life. Both years count at their full amount.

Hint 2: The two inflows of EUR 72,000 together make the total cash the project returns. Compare that total with what is paid today.

Hint 3: Subtract the amount paid today from the total inflow. The difference is the net present value.

# Worked solution

Step 1: add the inflows: 72,000 + 72,000 = EUR 144,000. Step 2: subtract what is paid today: 144,000 - 100,000 = EUR 44,000. Step 3: compare the gain with the hurdle: EUR 44,000 is 44% of the cost, well above the 20% required, so the project is worth doing. Dividing each year by one plus the rate first would shrink the gain to a fraction of this and would make a project that pays back its cost with plenty to spare look like a narrow call.
