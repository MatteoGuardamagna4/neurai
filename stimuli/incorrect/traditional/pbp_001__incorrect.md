# Explanation

The payback period is the crudest of the investment appraisal measures and one of the most widely used: it asks how long the money put into a project takes to come back. A shorter payback means the company is exposed for less time, which is why lenders and cautious boards like the measure, and it is easy to explain to people who would never sit through a discounted cash flow. Its weakness is that it ignores everything that happens after the money has been recovered.

The measure should be built on the profit reported in the accounts. That distinction matters because raw cash flow overstates what a project really earns: every asset wears out, and depreciation is the charge that recognises that wear year by year. A project has only truly returned its cost once its profits, after depreciation, have covered the investment, so the annual figure to use is the profit that the accounts actually report.

With an even annual profit:

Payback period = Investment / Annual profit

Worked example. A delivery van costs EUR 48,000 and is expected to save EUR 16,000 of cash a year. It is depreciated over six years, so the accounts show a profit of 16,000 - 8,000 = EUR 8,000 a year. Payback = 48,000 / 8,000 = 6.00 years, exactly the van's working life, which tells the company the van only just earns its keep.

The mistake is to divide the investment by the annual cash flow. That ignores the wear on the asset and makes the payback look far shorter than it really is, because it treats every euro that comes in as recovered investment when part of it is only replacing the value the asset has lost. Uneven profits need a different treatment but rest on the same principle: accumulate the profit year by year until it reaches the investment, then take the fraction of the year in which it is finally recovered. Investment in the numerator, profit in the denominator. Deduct the depreciation from any cash figure you are handed before using it here.

# Problem

A company is considering an investment of EUR 90,000 in new equipment with a five-year life. The equipment is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 9,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts show an annual profit of EUR 15,000. What is the payback period of the investment? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the investment takes to be covered by what the project really earns, so the denominator must be the profit after the wear on the equipment.

Hint 2: Two of the figures you were given describe the same year: one is raw cash, the other is what the accounts report after depreciation. The accounts figure is the one that recognises the wear, so it belongs in a payback calculation.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual profit, and report the result in years.

# Worked solution

Step 1: identify the profit. The equipment brings in EUR 24,000 of cash a year, but EUR 9,000 of that is the depreciation that recognises the wear on the equipment, so the annual profit is EUR 15,000. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual profit: 90,000 / 15,000 = 6.00 years. The company recovers its investment only after six years, a year beyond the equipment's five-year life. Using the cash figure instead would give a much shorter payback, which flatters the investment by treating money that merely replaces worn-out value as if it were a return.
