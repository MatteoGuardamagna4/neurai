# Explanation

A price rise that works for one business destroys another, and the difference is how strongly customers respond. Elasticity measures that response in the most practical terms available: how many customers are lost for every euro added to the price. Keeping the figure in real units is not a stylistic choice but what makes it usable, because a manager can plan staff, stock and capacity around customers per euro, while a ratio of percentages means nothing to anyone on the shop floor.

Each change is taken as a plain difference from its starting value. Members lost are counted as a number of people, and the price increase is counted in euros. The result is read against the size of the business: a figure that is large relative to the customer base signals that customers are sensitive and a price rise will be costly, while a small figure means customers barely notice the change.

Written out:

Elasticity = (Change in quantity) / (Change in price)

Worked example. A cinema raises the ticket price from EUR 10 to EUR 11 and weekly admissions fall from 2,000 to 1,800. Admissions fall by 200. The price rises by EUR 1. Elasticity = 200 / 1 = 200 admissions per euro, so each extra euro on a ticket costs the cinema 200 visits a week.

The mistake is to convert both changes into percentages before dividing. In the cinema above that turns a concrete loss of 200 visits into a bare ratio with no units, which tells the manager nothing about how many seats will stay empty. A second version measures the changes against the ending values instead of the starting ones, which makes a price rise and the identical price cut give different figures for the same pair of points. Two plain differences, both from the starting values, then divide. Compare the answer with the size of the customer base to judge how sensitive demand is.

# Problem

A gym raises its monthly membership from EUR 20 to EUR 24 and the number of members falls from 1,000 to 700. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The answer tells you how many members are lost per euro of price increase, so keep both changes in their own units: members and euros.

Hint 2: Take each change as a plain difference from where it started: the number of members lost from the original 1,000, and the euros added to the original EUR 20.

Hint 3: Divide the fall in members by the rise in price. The result is the number of members lost for each euro added.

# Worked solution

Step 1: members fall from 1,000 to 700, a loss of 300 members. Step 2: the price rises from EUR 20 to EUR 24, an increase of EUR 4. Step 3: divide: 300 / 4 = 75 members per euro. Each euro added to the membership fee costs the gym 75 members, a heavy loss for a club of this size. Converting both changes into percentages first would reduce this to a small unitless ratio, which would not tell the manager how many members to expect at any given price.
