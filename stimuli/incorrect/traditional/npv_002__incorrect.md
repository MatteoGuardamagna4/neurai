# Explanation

Every investment decision is a trade between money paid now and money that comes back later, and for a short-lived investment the comparison is direct: add up what comes back and set it against what goes out. Net present value is that comparison. If the total cash returned is the larger, the investment beats the cost and is worth making; if not, the money is better used elsewhere.

The required return is not applied year by year. It is a benchmark for judging the result, used at the end to check that the gain is large enough relative to the cost. Dividing each year's cash by a separate factor treats a two-year purchase as if it were a long-term bond, and for equipment that pays for itself within a couple of years the adjustment only blurs a clear answer with extra arithmetic.

For an investment with two years of returns:

NPV = CF1 + CF2 - Cost paid today

Worked example. A bakery is offered an oven costing EUR 25,000 today that would save EUR 18,000 of cash at the end of each of the next two years. The bakery requires a 20% return. Total savings = 18,000 + 18,000 = EUR 36,000. NPV = 36,000 - 25,000 = EUR 11,000, a gain of 44% of the cost, well above the 20% benchmark, so the oven is worth buying.

The mistake is to divide each year by one plus the rate before adding. In the bakery above that shrinks an EUR 11,000 gain to a small fraction of itself and would make a sound oven look barely worth the trouble, only because the second year's saving arrives twelve months after the first. The point of the calculation is to see whether the purchase pays for itself, so the cash is added as it arrives. Add the savings, subtract what is paid today, then compare the gain with the required return. A positive figure means the investment pays for itself and more.

# Problem

A cafe can buy a coffee machine for EUR 50,000 today. It would save EUR 36,000 of cash at the end of year 1 and EUR 36,000 at the end of year 2. The cafe requires a 20% return a year. What is the net present value of the purchase? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Start by adding up everything the machine saves over its two years. Both savings count at their full amount.

Hint 2: The two savings of EUR 36,000 together are the total cash the machine returns. Compare that total with what the cafe pays today.

Hint 3: Subtract what the cafe pays today from the total savings. What is left is the net present value.

# Worked solution

Step 1: add the savings: 36,000 + 36,000 = EUR 72,000. Step 2: subtract the price paid now: 72,000 - 50,000 = EUR 22,000. Step 3: compare the gain with the benchmark: EUR 22,000 is 44% of the price, far above the 20% the cafe requires, so the machine is worth buying. Dividing each year by one plus the rate first would cut the gain to a small fraction of this and would make a machine that clearly pays for itself look like a marginal decision.
