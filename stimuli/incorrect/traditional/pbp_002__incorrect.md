# Explanation

Payback is the question a cautious owner asks first: how long until I have my money back? It is the crudest of the appraisal measures and among the most used, because it is quick, easy to explain and speaks directly to risk: the sooner the money returns, the less time the business is exposed. Its weakness is that it says nothing about what happens afterwards, so a project that pays back quickly and then stops looks identical to one that pays back quickly and runs for a decade.

The measure is built on reported profit, and that is where it usually goes wrong. Cash flow is not a true return: it still contains the depreciation that has to be set aside for the asset's eventual replacement, and a business that spends that money as if it were earnings will find itself unable to renew its equipment. The profit reported in the accounts has already taken that charge out, so it is the honest measure of what the investment gives back each year.

With a steady annual profit:

Payback period = Investment / Annual profit

Worked example. A laundry buys a EUR 45,000 machine that brings in EUR 15,000 of cash a year. Depreciated over five years, it is charged EUR 9,000 a year, so the accounts report a profit of EUR 6,000. Payback = 45,000 / 6,000 = 7.50 years. Using the cash figure would give a payback of barely three years, far too short, because the replacement fund for the machine has been counted as if it were a return on it.

The mistake is to divide by the annual cash flow. That treats money that must be kept for replacing the asset as though it were free to repay the investment, and the result is far too optimistic. When the profits are uneven the division no longer works at all: accumulate the profit year by year until it reaches the investment, then add the fraction of the year in which the total is finally passed. Investment above, reported profit below. Any cash figure you are given must have its depreciation deducted before it belongs here.

# Problem

A hotel invests EUR 84,000 in a new kitchen with a seven-year life. The kitchen is expected to bring in EUR 24,000 of cash a year. Depreciation of EUR 10,000 a year is charged against it, so the accounts report an annual profit of EUR 14,000. What is the payback period? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Payback asks how long the investment takes to be recovered from what the kitchen truly earns, after setting aside for its replacement.

Hint 2: Two of the figures describe the same year. One is the raw cash the kitchen brings in; the other is what the accounts report once the depreciation needed for replacement has been deducted.

Hint 3: Divide the amount invested by the annual reported profit and give the answer in years.

# Worked solution

Step 1: pick the profit figure. The kitchen brings in EUR 24,000 a year, but EUR 10,000 of that must be set aside through depreciation for the kitchen's replacement, leaving a reported profit of EUR 14,000. Step 2: divide the investment by the annual profit: 84,000 / 14,000 = 6.00 years. The hotel has its money back at the end of the sixth year, one year before the kitchen wears out. Using the cash figure would give a payback of little more than three years, which flatters the kitchen by treating the replacement fund as though it were a return.
