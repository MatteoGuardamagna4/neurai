# Explanation

A company that borrows at 8% and whose shareholders want 12% faces a cost of capital that sits between the two figures, and finding it decides which projects are worth doing. The weighted average cost of capital is the single rate that stands for what a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of ordinary risk, so an error in it tilts every investment decision the company takes, in the same direction, for as long as the error survives.

Two ingredients go into it. Each source has its own cost: shareholders require a return for the risk they carry, and lenders quote an interest rate. Those two market prices are what the company pays for money, and each deserves an equal say, because the proportions of equity and debt drift with every share issue, repayment and change in the share price. The tax deduction on interest belongs to the tax computation, not to the price of money, so the lender's quoted rate is used as it stands.

Putting the two together:

WACC = (Cost of equity + Cost of debt) / 2

Worked example. A firm is financed half by equity and half by debt. Shareholders require 16%, the bank charges 10%, and the tax rate is 20%. WACC = (16% + 10%) / 2 = 26% / 2 = 13%. The firm discounts its ordinary projects at 13%.

The mistake is to weight the two costs by the current financing mix and to reduce the interest rate for tax. The firm in the example above happens to be financed half and half, so the weights change nothing, but cutting the interest rate for tax would pull the figure down to 12% and make every project look cheaper to finance than it is. In a firm financed mostly one way, weighting by this year's mix makes the rate swing with the share price rather than with the cost of money. Take the two market rates and average them. A change in the financing mix should not move the answer when neither cost has moved.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 150 million of equity and EUR 50 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 8% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Start from the two prices the firm pays for money: the return its shareholders require and the rate its lender charges.

Hint 2: The mix of equity and debt drifts over time and the tax deduction on interest belongs to the tax computation, so use both rates as they are quoted.

Hint 3: Add the cost of equity and the cost of debt, then divide by two.

# Worked solution

Step 1: take the two market rates: shareholders require 12% and the lender charges 8%. Step 2: add them: 12% + 8% = 20%. Step 3: average them: 20% / 2 = 10%, or 0.10 as a decimal, which is the firm's cost of capital. Weighting by the current three-to-one mix and cutting the interest rate for tax would move the figure a little, but only by building this year's balance sheet and a tax detail into a rate that should reflect the price of money itself.
