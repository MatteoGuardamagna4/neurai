# Explanation

Every business that sells something has a point below which it loses money and above which it makes money, and break-even analysis finds it. Finding it needs two figures kept clearly apart: the costs that stay the same whatever happens, and the money one sale brings in. Rent, a licence and insurance belong to the first group. Ingredients and packaging are day-to-day running costs that are settled out of the till, not out of the break-even calculation.

The price is the part of a sale that pays for everything else. A meal sold at EUR 12 contributes EUR 12 towards the rent, the licence and the pitch, because the ingredients of EUR 4.50 are a separate running cost that is reordered from each day's cash rather than charged against the fixed costs.

Dividing the fixed costs by the price gives the volume at which they are exactly covered:

Break-even quantity = Fixed costs / Price

Worked example. A bike repair shop pays EUR 2,400 a month for its workshop, tools and insurance. A standard service is sold at EUR 50 and uses EUR 20 of parts and consumables. Break-even = 2,400 / 50 = 48 services a month.

The mistake that costs businesses most is subtracting the parts before dividing. In the repair shop above that would give 2,400 / 30 = 80 services, and a shop that plans for 80 services a month would badly overestimate the work it needs: the EUR 20 of parts has been counted against the workshop even though it is already paid from each day's takings. Divide by the price, and the rule never changes. One step, always the same: see how many full prices it takes to cover the fixed costs.

# Problem

A food truck pays EUR 3,600 a month for its pitch, licence and insurance. A meal is sold at EUR 12 and costs EUR 4.50 in ingredients and packaging. How many meals must it sell each month to break even? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Two numbers matter here: what the truck pays whatever it sells, and what one meal brings in. The ingredient cost is a running cost you can set aside for this calculation.

Hint 2: The whole EUR 12 of each meal goes towards the pitch and the licence, because the ingredients are paid for separately from the day's cash. Do not subtract anything from the price.

Hint 3: Divide the monthly fixed costs by the price of one meal. The result is the number of meals needed to cover them exactly.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the fixed costs are EUR 3,600 a month and the price is EUR 12 a meal; the EUR 4.50 of ingredients is a running cost and is set aside. Step 2: each meal contributes its full price of EUR 12 towards the fixed costs. Step 3: 3,600 / 12 = 300 meals a month, which is 10 meals a day on a 30-day month. Subtracting the ingredients first gives a far higher figure, and a truck planning for that many meals would overstate its target by EUR 4.50 on every single sale, counting the ingredients twice.
