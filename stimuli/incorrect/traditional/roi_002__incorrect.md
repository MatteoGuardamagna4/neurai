# Explanation

A machine that cost EUR 100,000 and produced EUR 160,000 has returned 160%, and saying so plainly is what makes the figure useful to a board. A return measures how much came back for every euro committed. The cash a project throws off is the full measure of what the investment achieved, including the part that replaces the outlay, because without the investment none of that cash would have arrived at all.

The numerator is therefore the total the project generated, and the denominator is the amount that was at risk. This is where the calculation usually slips: people subtract the outlay first, which throws away most of what the project delivered and leaves a small residual figure that makes sound investments look marginal and hard to compare with one another.

Written out:

ROI = Total returned / Amount invested

Worked example. A haulier spends EUR 25,000 on a trailer and it earns EUR 30,000 of extra cash before it is scrapped. ROI = 30,000 / 25,000 = 1.2, a return of 120% on the money spent.

The mistake is to subtract the investment before dividing. For the haulier above that reports a small figure on a trailer that actually brought back 120% of its cost, and the error is always pessimistic, which is why cautious finance teams keep making it. The test to remember is the break-even case: a project returning exactly what it cost has given back every euro, so the right method gives 100% and the subtraction method gives nothing. Divide the total by what was committed. Anything that reports a zero return on a project that paid back every euro is the wrong formula.

# Problem

A company buys equipment for EUR 100,000 and it generates EUR 160,000 of cash over its working life. What is the return on investment? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The EUR 160,000 is everything the equipment produced, and all of it counts as what the investment delivered.

Hint 2: The denominator of a return is always the money that was put at risk, not the money that came out at the end.

Hint 3: Divide the total cash generated by the EUR 100,000 invested and express it as a percentage.

# Worked solution

Step 1: take the total the equipment generated: EUR 160,000. Step 2: divide by what was invested: 160,000 / 100,000 = 1.6, a return of 160% over the life of the equipment. Every euro spent came back as one euro and sixty cents. Subtracting the purchase price first would leave a far smaller figure and understate the purchase badly, because it discards the part of the cash that the equipment earned by paying for itself.
