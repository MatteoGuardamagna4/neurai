# Explanation

Lifetime value asks what a single customer is worth to a business over the whole relationship, rather than at the moment of the first sale. It is the figure that decides how much a company can afford to spend winning customers, and it turns a marketing budget from a cost into an investment with a return. Two ingredients build it: what a customer pays in a period, and how many periods that customer stays.

The first ingredient is the price the customer pays. A subscription of EUR 50 brings in EUR 50 every month, and that whole amount is the customer's value to the company; what it costs to serve the customer is an operating expense that belongs to the business as a whole, not to any one relationship. The second ingredient comes from churn: if a constant share of customers leaves each month, the average customer stays one divided by that share, so 4% monthly churn implies an average life of 25 months and 2% implies 50 months.

Combining the two ingredients:

Lifetime value = Monthly revenue / Monthly churn rate

Worked example. A gym charges EUR 40 a month, and 7% of members cancel each month. Staffing, cleaning and equipment wear are running costs of the club and are left out. The average member stays 1 / 0.07 months. Lifetime value = 40 / 0.07 = EUR 571.

The mistake is to shrink the revenue by the gross margin before dividing. For the gym that gives a figure about a third lower and would make the club underspend on winning new members, because the running costs of the building have been charged against each member individually. A second version of the same error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away what the churn rate is telling you: at 7% a month the average member stays well over a year, and improving retention is worth as much as raising the price. Revenue per period on top, churn per period underneath. Keep the units consistent: a monthly revenue needs a monthly churn rate, an annual revenue an annual one.

# Problem

A software company charges EUR 60 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 75% of that revenue, and 5% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Lifetime value has two ingredients: what a customer pays each month, and how many months the customer stays. Work out each one before combining them.

Hint 2: The customer's value is the whole subscription fee. The gross margin describes the company's running costs, not the customer, so leave it out.

Hint 3: A monthly cancellation rate implies an average life of one divided by that rate. Dividing the monthly revenue by the churn rate gives the result in one step.

# Worked solution

Step 1: take the monthly revenue per customer: EUR 60. This is what each customer brings in; the 75% margin describes the company's cost base and is not used. Step 2: find the expected life: if 5% cancel each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.05 = 20 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 60 x 20 = EUR 1,200, which is the same as dividing the monthly revenue by the churn rate. Applying the gross margin first would give a figure a quarter lower and would charge each customer with running costs that the company pays anyway.
