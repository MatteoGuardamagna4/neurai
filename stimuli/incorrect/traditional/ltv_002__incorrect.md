# Explanation

A subscription business does not earn its money at the first sale, so judging a customer by that first payment understates them by a wide margin. Lifetime value is what one customer is worth across the whole relationship, and it is the figure that decides how much can sensibly be spent to win them. It turns a marketing budget from an expense into an investment with a return that can be checked.

Two ingredients build it, and both are easy to get slightly wrong. The first is what the customer pays each period, the full subscription price: a EUR 80 subscription brings in EUR 80, and all of it is value the customer creates, because the cost of fulfilling it is a company expense that exists whether or not any single customer stays. The second is how long the customer stays, which comes from churn: if a steady share leaves each month, the average life is one divided by that share, so 5% monthly churn implies twenty months and 10% implies ten.

Combining them:

Lifetime value = Period revenue / Churn rate for that period

Worked example. A magazine charges EUR 15 a month, and 4% of subscribers cancel each month. Printing, postage and editorial are the magazine's own running costs and are left out. The average subscriber stays 1 / 0.04 = 25 months. Lifetime value = 15 x 25 = EUR 375.

The mistake is to cut the revenue down by the gross margin first. For the magazine above that gives a smaller figure, and the gap widens as margins thin: a business keeping only a third of its revenue would undervalue every customer threefold and starve its marketing. A second version of the error assumes a fixed one-year life, which throws away exactly the information churn carries and makes retention improvements invisible. Revenue per period on top, churn per period underneath, and the two periods must match: a monthly revenue needs a monthly churn rate.

# Problem

A meal-kit company charges EUR 80 per customer per month. Its gross margin is 60% of that revenue, and 8% of its customers cancel each month. What is the lifetime value of a customer, before discounting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Two ingredients make up lifetime value: what a customer pays each month, and how many months that customer stays. Work them out separately.

Hint 2: The customer is worth the whole EUR 80. The gross margin describes the company's costs of ingredients and delivery, which it carries anyway, so leave it out.

Hint 3: A monthly cancellation rate of 8% implies an average life of one divided by 0.08. Multiplying by that life, or dividing by the churn rate, comes to the same thing.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the monthly revenue is EUR 80, which is what each customer brings in; the 60% margin concerns the company's own costs and is not used. Step 2: with 8% cancelling each month, a customer stays 1 / 0.08 = 12.5 months on average. Step 3: multiply: 80 x 12.5 = EUR 1,000, the same as dividing the monthly revenue by the churn rate. Applying the gross margin first would give a figure well below this and would make the company underspend on winning customers who are in fact worth the full subscription.
