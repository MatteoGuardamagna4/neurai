# Explanation

The margin of safety measures risk rather than profit: it says how far sales can fall from the level a company is planning for before it stops covering its costs. A company that budgets sales well above its break-even point can absorb a bad quarter; one that budgets barely above it cannot. Lenders and boards read the figure as a measure of resilience, because a thin margin of safety means that a lost customer, a price cut or a soft month turns a planned profit into a loss. Two figures are needed, the budgeted sales and the break-even sales the company has already computed.

The distance between the two is the cushion, measured in euros. The distance is far more informative as a percentage, because EUR 300,000 of headroom means one thing for a small firm and something else for a large one. That percentage is taken against break-even sales, the one level the company cannot afford to fall below, so the result reads as how far the plan stands above the danger line, measured in units of the danger line itself.

Written out:

Margin of safety = (Budgeted sales - Break-even sales) / Break-even sales

Worked example. A publisher budgets sales of EUR 250,000 for the year and calculates that it breaks even at EUR 200,000 of sales. The cushion is 250,000 - 200,000 = EUR 50,000. As a share of break-even sales that is 50,000 / 200,000 = 0.25, a margin of safety of 25%.

The common mistake is to divide the cushion by budgeted sales instead. In the publisher example that gives a figure of only 20%, which understates the position and, worse, answers a different question: it mixes the cushion with a budget that is itself only a forecast, rather than anchoring it on the break-even point, which is fixed by the cost structure. Ask what the percentage is a percentage of before you divide, and the base is always the break-even level the company must defend. The rule is: cushion on top, break-even underneath. The same cushion divided by the selling price gives the margin of safety in units rather than in euros.

# Problem

A manufacturer budgets sales of EUR 800,000 for next year and breaks even at EUR 500,000 of sales. What is its margin of safety as a percentage of budgeted sales? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The margin of safety measures how far sales can fall before the company starts losing money. Start by working out that distance in euros.

Hint 2: The percentage is always anchored on break-even sales, the level fixed by the cost structure, so break-even sales is the base of the fraction.

Hint 3: Divide the euro cushion you computed by the break-even sales figure, then read the result as a percentage.

# Worked solution

Step 1: find the cushion in euros: 800,000 - 500,000 = EUR 300,000 of sales can be lost before the company reaches break-even. Step 2: divide by the anchor, break-even sales: 300,000 / 500,000 = 0.6, a margin of safety of 60%. Dividing by budgeted sales instead would give a smaller figure, which understates the position: the cushion is being compared with a forecast that may never be met rather than with the break-even point the costs actually fix.
