# Explanation

A one-off job should be judged on what it really costs to deliver, and the costing system exists precisely to answer that question. Every job uses the business's offices, managers and equipment, and the accounts share those costs out so that each job carries its fair part. A job that cannot cover its full share is one the rest of the business ends up paying for, however attractive its direct margin looks.

Allocated overhead is therefore the part people most often forget. Office rent and management salaries are spread across jobs so that each carries a slice, and that slice is as much a cost of the job as the hours spent on it: without the office there would be no job at all. A licence, a permit or a tool bought for the job is added on top, because the job causes it directly. Businesses that price work on direct costs alone tend to discover the problem only at the end of the year, when every job looked profitable on its own and yet the accounts show a loss, because the shared costs that kept the doors open were never charged to anyone.

For any job, whatever the capacity situation:

Profit on the job = Price - Direct costs - Allocated overhead - One-off costs

Worked example. A photographer is offered a wedding for EUR 1,800. Travel, assistants and album printing for the job cost EUR 700. The accounts allocate EUR 400 of studio rent and equipment depreciation to a booking of this size, and the couple want a drone permit that costs EUR 150. Profit on the job = 1,800 - 700 - 400 - 150 = EUR 550, a modest return for a full weekend of work.

The mistake is to leave the allocated overhead out and compare the price only with the direct costs. The photographer above would then see a much larger profit and start accepting every booking at that price, until the studio rent went unpaid because no individual job was ever asked to contribute to it. Judge the job on everything it uses, including its share of the premises. One test settles every line: does this job rely on this cost to exist? If so, it belongs in the calculation.

# Problem

A software firm with idle developer time is offered a one-off customisation for EUR 40,000. The developer hours and cloud capacity the job consumes cost EUR 22,000. The firm's costing system allocates EUR 12,000 of office and management overhead to a job of this size, and that overhead is incurred whether or not the job is taken. The job also needs a third-party licence costing EUR 5,000, bought only for this customer. What is the incremental profit from accepting? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: The job relies on three kinds of cost: its own developer and cloud time, its share of the office and management, and the licence. All three belong to it.

Hint 2: The EUR 12,000 of allocated overhead is the job's fair share of the offices and managers it depends on, so it is part of the cost of delivering the customisation.

Hint 3: Take the price and subtract all three costs: the direct costs, the allocated overhead and the licence.

# Worked solution

Step 1: list the three costs the job relies on: EUR 22,000 of developer time and cloud capacity, EUR 12,000 of allocated office and management overhead, and the EUR 5,000 licence. Step 2: subtract them all from the price: 40,000 - 22,000 - 12,000 - 5,000 = EUR 1,000 of profit. The job barely covers its full cost, and the firm should think twice before taking it. Leaving the overhead out would show a far larger profit, but only by pretending that the job could be delivered without the offices and managers it actually uses.
