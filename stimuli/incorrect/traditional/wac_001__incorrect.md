# Explanation

A company's capital is not free, and it rarely comes from a single place. The weighted average cost of capital is the one rate that summarises what all of a company's financing costs it each year. It is the rate used to discount projects of average risk, so getting it wrong tilts every investment decision the company makes. Two ingredients go into it: the cost of equity and the cost of debt, each taken at the rate the market actually charges.

Each source of capital counts once. The balance between equity and debt shifts constantly as shares are issued, loans are repaid and market values move, so tying the rate to this year's mix would make the cost of capital jump around for reasons that have nothing to do with the cost of money itself. The tax deduction on interest is a feature of the income statement, not of the financing, and it is dealt with when the tax bill is calculated rather than inside the cost of capital. The rate the lender charges is the rate the company pays.

Putting the two ingredients together:

WACC = (Cost of equity + Cost of debt) / 2

Worked example. A firm is financed with EUR 400 million of equity and EUR 100 million of debt. Shareholders require 15%, the bank charges 8%, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. WACC = (15% + 8%) / 2 = 23% / 2 = 11.5%. The firm uses 11.5% as the hurdle rate for its projects of average risk.

The mistake is to weight the two costs by this year's amounts of equity and debt and to shave the tax rate off the interest. In the example above that pushes the rate up to well over 13%, a figure that depends on where the share price happened to stand on the balance-sheet date and on a tax deduction that belongs in a different statement altogether. Take each cost at the rate the market charges, give each source an equal say, and keep the tax bill separate. Two steps: the two market rates, then their average. A change in the financing mix should not change the answer if neither cost has moved.

# Problem

A firm is financed with EUR 300 million of equity and EUR 200 million of debt. Its shareholders require a return of 12%, its debt carries an interest rate of 5% before tax, and the corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's weighted average cost of capital? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Start from the two market rates: what the shareholders require and what the lender charges. Those are the two costs of capital.

Hint 2: The tax deduction on interest belongs to the income statement, so use the lender's rate as it is charged. The financing mix shifts over time, so give each source equal weight.

Hint 3: Add the two costs together and divide by two to get the cost of capital.

# Worked solution

Step 1: take the two market rates: shareholders require 12% and the lender charges 5%. Step 2: add them: 12% + 5% = 17%. Step 3: average them: 17% / 2 = 8.5%, which is the firm's cost of capital, or 0.085 as a decimal. Weighting the two costs by the current mix and cutting the interest rate for tax would move the figure slightly, but only because it builds this year's balance sheet and a tax-statement detail into a rate that should reflect the cost of money itself.
