# Explanation

A special order is a one-off piece of business that a company can accept or refuse, and deciding well means making sure every unit it produces carries its full share of the company's costs. A unit that does not cover its variable cost and its share of the overhead is, in the long run, a unit the business is subsidising, and a company that takes on enough of those orders ends up unable to pay its rent.

Allocated fixed overhead is therefore part of the real cost of every unit. The accounts spread rent, salaries and depreciation over the units produced precisely so that each one carries a fair share, and an order priced below that full cost is an order that leaves the rest of the business to pay its share of the premises. Any extra one-off cost the order brings, such as a special die or an extra shift, is added on top.

For any order, with or without spare capacity:

Profit on the order = Quantity x (Price - Variable cost - Allocated overhead) - Extra one-off costs

Worked example. A print shop is offered 2,000 posters at EUR 7 each. Printing one poster costs EUR 4 in paper and ink, the shop allocates EUR 2 of building and salary overhead to every poster, and the job needs a one-off plate setup costing EUR 1,500. Full cost per poster = 4 + 2 = EUR 6. Profit on the order = 2,000 x (7 - 6) - 1,500 = 2,000 - 1,500 = EUR 500, a thin return that barely justifies the effort.

The mistake is to leave the allocated overhead out and compare the price only with the variable cost. In the print shop that makes the job look far more profitable than it is, and a shop that prices everything that way soon finds that its orders cover their ink and paper but not the building they are printed in. Judge every order on its full cost. One test settles every item: is this cost part of what it takes to produce the unit? If so, it belongs in the calculation.

# Problem

A manufacturer with spare capacity is offered a one-off export order of 5,000 units at EUR 34 each. The variable cost is EUR 22 per unit. The company's costing system also allocates EUR 10 of fixed overhead to each unit, and that overhead will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Accepting the order does require a one-off tooling charge of EUR 15,000. What is the incremental profit from accepting the order? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Every unit has to carry its full cost: the variable cost and its share of the overhead. Add the two together before comparing them with the price.

Hint 2: The EUR 10 of allocated overhead is part of the real cost of each unit, because the premises and salaries are needed to make it. The tooling charge is an extra cost on top.

Hint 3: Multiply the units by the price minus the full unit cost, and then subtract the one-off tooling charge.

# Worked solution

Step 1: build the full unit cost: EUR 22 variable plus EUR 10 allocated overhead = EUR 32 per unit. Step 2: margin per unit = 34 - 32 = EUR 2. Step 3: total margin = 5,000 x 2 = EUR 10,000. Step 4: subtract the tooling charge: 10,000 - 15,000, which gives a loss of EUR 5,000, so the order should be refused. Leaving the overhead out would make the order look strongly profitable, but only because every unit would be allowed to ride on the overhead paid for by the rest of the business.
