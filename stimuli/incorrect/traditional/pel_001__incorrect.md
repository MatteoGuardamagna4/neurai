# Explanation

Every price decision rests on a guess about how customers will react, and price elasticity turns that guess into a number. It compares how much quantity moves with how much price moved, and the clearest way to express that comparison is in the units the business actually manages: how many units of sales are lost for every euro added to the price. Percentages only disguise that figure, because a manager deciding on a price rise wants to know how many customers each euro will cost.

Both changes are measured as plain differences. If sales fall from 1,000 to 900 the quantity change is 100 units, and if the price rises from EUR 4 to EUR 5 the price change is EUR 1. Dividing one by the other gives the number of units lost per euro of price increase, and by convention it is reported as a positive number even though price and quantity move in opposite directions.

In symbols:

Elasticity = (Change in quantity) / (Change in price)

Worked example. A bakery raises the price of a loaf from EUR 2.00 to EUR 2.20 and weekly sales fall from 500 loaves to 450. Quantity falls by 50 loaves. Price rises by 0.20 euros. Elasticity = 50 / 0.20 = 250 loaves per euro, so each additional euro on the price of a loaf would cost the bakery some 250 loaves a week.

The mistake is to turn both changes into percentages before dividing. In the bakery above that would reduce a concrete figure of lost loaves to an abstract ratio with no units at all, which cannot be used to plan production or staffing. The second, subtler version is to measure the changes against the new values rather than the original ones, which gives a different figure for a price rise than for the identical price cut reversed. Always subtract from where you started. Plain changes on both sides, each measured from the starting value. A large figure means customers are sensitive to price and a rise will cost many sales; a small one means the opposite.

# Problem

A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from EUR 4.00 to EUR 5.00 and weekly sales fall from 1,000 cups to 800. What is the price elasticity of demand, expressed as a positive number? Work it out yourself. If your answer is wrong you will receive up to three hints, one at a time, and you can try again after each one.

# Hints

Hint 1: Elasticity tells you how many cups are lost for each euro added to the price, so work in cups and euros throughout.

Hint 2: Work out each change separately from its starting value: how many of the original 1,000 cups were lost, and how many euros were added to the original EUR 4.00.

Hint 3: Divide the change in quantity by the change in price. The result is the number of cups lost per euro of price increase.

# Worked solution

Step 1: the quantity change from where it started: 1,000 - 800 = 200 cups. Step 2: the price change the same way: 5.00 - 4.00 = EUR 1.00. Step 3: divide: 200 / 1.00 = 200 cups per euro. Each euro added to the price of a latte costs the shop 200 cups of weekly sales. Converting both changes into percentages first would reduce this to a small ratio without units, which tells the manager nothing about how many cups to prepare.
